Is Disability a Fact About Bodies, or Something Power Made?

Philosophy has treated disability as a plain biological disadvantage, too obvious to be worth a philosopher’s time. Tremain argues that this is false, that it was produced by a specific machinery of power from the eighteenth century on, and that philosophy helped build it. Her evidence for the last part is the profession itself: disabled philosophers are about two per cent of it.

If disability is a natural fact, philosophy has nothing to say about it. Tremain says it is not a natural fact.

The claim that does all the work There is no such thing as an impairment lying under a disability, waiting to be discovered. Impairment was produced by the same machinery that produced disability, and produced as a bad thing, a defect, something to be corrected.

What philosophy has been doing, and why she calls it a problem. Open any mainstream philosophy paper on disability and the questions run like this. What does society owe disabled people? How should it compensate them for their natural disadvantage and their bad luck? When is euthanasia permissible? Every one of those questions takes for granted that disability is a known quantity: a biological fact that medicine can describe accurately, the same in every century and every country, and a disadvantage. On that assumption there is nothing for a philosopher to investigate about what disability is. There is only the ethics of what to do about it.

Her reply is that the assumption is itself the thing to investigate. She argues that disability is an apparatus, a word she takes from Foucault, meaning a loose machine made of laws, clinics, statistics, school rules, census categories, research programmes and philosophy papers, which came together in Europe from about the eighteenth century and sorts people. On that account nobody has a disability, the way nobody has a race. People are placed, by the machine, on one side of a line it drew.

And then the part that makes the book unusual. Most philosophers writing about power stop at the argument. Tremain goes and counts. Disabled philosophers are about two per cent of the profession, against twenty-two per cent of the general population, and she argues the two facts are connected: a discipline that teaches disability as a natural defect will not see a disabled person as a colleague.

Three accounts of disability, and the week turns on telling them apart. The guide uses her names for them throughout.

  1. The medical or individualist account. Disability is a functional limitation of a particular person. The response is to adjust the environment a little so that person can be accommodated into it, with the environment otherwise left as it was.
  2. The British social model, from the United Kingdom in the 1970s. Impairment is a neutral fact about a body. Disability is the oppression society imposes on people who have one. The response is to change society.
  3. Disability as an apparatus, which is hers. Impairment is not neutral and not prior. The machine produced it, and produced it as a defect, so there is no untouched biological layer underneath for the other two accounts to stand on.

What she is attacking in the second one, which is the live argument of the week: the British social model keeps a natural fact at the bottom. Her charge is that putting a natural fact at the bottom is what lets philosophy go on treating the whole subject as settled biology.

Two readings, one question. Chapter 1 states the position and counts the profession. Chapter 2 supplies the Foucault that the position rests on, which is why it reads as a different kind of writing.

What you get out of this chapter. The three accounts of disability with the difference between them stated, which the field itself keeps confusing. What philosophy assumes before it starts

The question What is already decided by the time a philosopher asks what society owes disabled people?

The answer: that disability is a defect, that medicine can describe it accurately, and that it is the same thing everywhere. Tremain lists the questions mainstream philosophy actually asks. What does society owe disabled people? How should it compensate them for their natural disadvantage and their brute bad luck? On what grounds is euthanasia justifiable? Is experimenting on cognitively disabled subjects permissible?

Read the nouns in them. Natural disadvantage. Bad luck. Each one answers in advance the question of what disability is: a property of a person, non-accidental, biological, and bad. Nothing in the list asks whether that is true.

Her name for what this does. It casts disability as self-evident and therefore philosophically uninteresting, something science and medicine represent accurately and philosophy need only respond to ethically. And if the inequalities disabled people face follow from a biological fact, then they belong to medicine, and a philosophy department has no business with them.

Old and new biosocial science, which is the cleanest way into her position. She takes the pair from Dorothy E. Roberts.

What it saysWhat follows
The old biosocial science Biological differences produce social inequality Fix the bodies. Social problems come from oppressed people's biology, so intervene on the biology
The new biosocial science Social inequality produces biological differences There is no natural body. Every cell and everything that happens to it is affected by society, and genes determine nothing on their own

The claim she takes from the second row, in Roberts's words: all of life is at once biological and social, and biology is constituted by its interactions with the environment, where the older picture had it as a separate thing that then meets one. Brains are plastic. A plastic brain is a situated brain.

Phineas Gage, and what a hundred and seventy years did to him

The question Why spend four pages of a philosophy book on a railway worker from 1848?

The story as everybody is taught it. Gage was a capable, well-liked railway foreman in Vermont. In 1848 he was tamping blasting powder into a drill hole with a metre-long iron rod when the charge went off early and fired the rod through his head. It entered under his left cheekbone, passed through the front of his brain, and left through the top of his skull, landing some twenty-five metres away. He was speaking within minutes, and he lived twelve more years. He came out of it a different man: foul-mouthed, with no respect for anybody, unable to hold a plan, a shiftless drunk who died young. The moral drawn from it is that the front of the brain holds a person's character, and damaging it destroys who they are.

How far that story travels. Two-thirds of introductory psychology textbooks carry it. A neurologist at Harvard calls the case ground zero for the relationship between damage to a specific part of the brain and a change in behaviour, and says it helped establish brain science as a field.

What philosophers want from it. The question of personal identity asks what makes somebody at fifty the same person as the child they were. One answer is a continuous body, another is a continuous psychology: the memories, values and character. Gage is offered as the case where the body stays and the character goes, which is evidence for the second answer and for the claim that character lives in a piece of tissue.

What the record actually supports. The doctor everybody cites, John Martyn Harlow, treated Gage for a few months and reported that his friends found him no longer Gage. That is close to the whole of the contemporary evidence. The drunk, the brute and the shiftless wanderer were added over the following century by people who never met him. Malcolm Macmillan, who wrote the book on Gage, allows that there was a personality change and says it lasted two or three years. Gage then went to Chile and worked as a long-distance stagecoach driver, a job that needs skill, concentration and the ability to get on with passengers. He died in San Francisco in 1860, aged thirty-six, of a series of seizures, which is what a brain injury of that kind produces years later. No contemporary record says he drank.

So the invented details are the load-bearing ones. Take away the drunk and the brute and the personal identity argument has nothing to stand on, because a man who was irritable for two years and then drove stagecoaches is not evidence that an injury destroyed a self. Philosophy built conclusions on details nobody checked for a century.

Why she puts it first. Because it is a case where you can watch the thing being made. Science, medicine and philosophy are practices people carry out, and an ableist assumption went into this one and came out the other side as a fact in a textbook. Her questions to the field: in what ways has this story about Gage confirmed what philosophers already believed about disabled people, and what has repeating it done to who feels welcome in a philosophy department?

Three accounts of disability, and why the field keeps mixing them up

The question When two authors both say they hold “the social model”, what might each of them mean?

The answer: two different things, and the confusion between them is why a lot of the literature does not add up. Tremain says the term social model gets used at two levels at once.

  1. Broadly, to mean any account of disability that is not the medical one. That is a whole family of positions.
  2. Narrowly, to mean one particular member of that family: the British social model, which came out of the United Kingdom in the 1970s.

The technical name for the mistake. Confusing a type with a token of that type, which is the distinction between a kind of thing and a particular instance of it. Saying “I drive a Toyota” and “I drive a car” are claims at different levels, and an argument that slides between them proves nothing.

The British social model, set out properly, because it is the position the week is arguing against.

  • Impairment is a neutral fact about a body. A missing limb, a condition, a difference. Neutral means it carries no judgment on its own.
  • Disability is the oppression that society imposes on people who have one: steps with no ramp, employers who will not hire, benefits withheld.
  • It is built on a parallel with sex and gender. Impairment is to disability what sex is to gender: the body underneath, and the social arrangement built on top.
  • Its politics is systemic. Grounded in historical materialism, it says improving disabled people's circumstances needs social, political and economic change, and never adjustments made one person at a time.

The individualist, or medical, account. Disability is a functional limitation that a particular person has. The response is accommodation: adjust the workplace a little so this person fits into it, and leave the workplace otherwise as it was. Tremain's objection is that it fits liberal individualism exactly and does not escape the eugenic push of the medical model it claims to replace.

Why the mixing matters for a sentence you will meet. When an author uses disabled people and people with disabilities as though they were interchangeable, they have run two incompatible positions together. The first phrase belongs to the social model, where people are disabled by an arrangement. The second belongs to the individualist one, where a person carries a disability around.

Her own position: disability is an apparatus

The question If impairment is not the natural thing underneath disability, what is it?

The answer: a product of the same machinery, made to look like the thing underneath. This is the sentence the whole book rests on, and it is worth reading twice. Impairment is both an effect of the apparatus of disability and a mechanism of it. The machine produced impairment, produced it as a defect, and then points at it as the natural fact that justifies everything the machine does.

What an apparatus is, in Foucault's own definition from 1980, which chapter 2 unpacks properly. A thoroughly mixed and interconnected collection of discourses, institutions, buildings, regulations, laws, scientific statements, administrative measures and moral propositions, which comes together in response to an urgent need at a particular moment in history.

What belongs to this one. Medical and scientific research, government policy, administrative decisions, academic work, activism, art, literature, popular culture. Parts of it contradict each other and it still holds together.

Nobody has a disability, and saying somebody does is a category mistake Her parallel: people are racialised by the apparatus of race, and nobody has a race. People are sexed by the apparatus of sex, and nobody has a sex. So people are disabled or not disabled by the apparatus of disability, and nobody has a disability. On this account the phrase a person with a disability names something that does not exist. The terms are disabled people and nondisabled people, and both are positions the machine puts you in.

And nobody is outside it. That males are also sexed, and white people also racialised, is the point of the parallel. Not being subjected as disabled does not put you outside the apparatus; it puts you in its other position.

Which classifications she treats as products of the machine, and this is a list to have, because each one sounds like a plain description: visible against invisible disabilities, mental against psychological, physical against cognitive, severe against moderate against mild, high functioning against low functioning. Her claim is that every one of those pairs puts disability back inside the individual as a personal attribute.

What the profession actually looks like

The question What happens to a discipline's membership when it teaches that one kind of person is naturally defective?

Her answer is a set of numbers, and they are the most usable thing in the chapter. Philosophy, she says, is the most homogeneous discipline in the humanities and social sciences, rivalled only by the sciences and engineering.

GroupShare of full-time philosophy faculty
WomenAbout 25 per cent in the United States, 30 to 35 per cent in Canada, about 25 per cent in Australia

Disabled philosophersAbout 2 per cent, by an American Philosophical Association survey. Under 1 per cent in Canada, by the Canadian Philosophical Association in 2013

The comparison to hold. Twenty-two per cent of the population, two per cent of the profession.

And the same pattern across the university. A freedom of information request at the University of California at Berkeley found 24 of 1,552 full-time faculty were disabled, about 1.5 per cent.

The experiment, which is the piece of evidence to cite. Researchers at Rutgers and Syracuse sent out identical résumés and cover letters for accounting jobs. The only difference was that some cover letters said the applicant was disabled. Employers expressed interest 26 per cent less often in the disabled applicants. For experienced candidates the gap was 34 per cent; for candidates at the start of their careers, 15 per cent.

What that produces outside the university. Thirty-four per cent of working-age disabled people are employed, against 74 per cent of nondisabled people. Most of those employed are in low-paying work. In 2014, 28.2 per cent of disabled people in the United States lived in poverty, against 13 per cent of nondisabled people, and the gap widens once the extra housing and transport costs many disabled people carry are counted.

Her argument about accommodation, which applies to every university including this one. Most campuses run an accommodationist policy: the building and the timetable are designed for one group, whose requirements count as standard and whose costs are invisible because nobody calls them costs. Anybody else has special needs, which are extra, which are granted on request, and which almost always need a doctor's certificate first.

So the effort is unequal, and that is the whole of the objection. One group gets what it needs by the building existing. The other has to medicalise its situation and work through an administration to get the equivalent.

Two things she connects it to. Rawls set the claims of disabled people aside in A Theory of Justice, to be dealt with after the basic structure had been settled for normal and cooperating people. And the reasonable accommodation standard that the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 is built around is, on her reading, a limiting device: it fixes how far anybody is allowed to ask.

Justice is blind, and why she will not let it pass

The question Some ableist metaphors are plainly insults. Is justice is blind one of them?

Where this comes from. Tremain has been arguing that philosophy is ableist in what it says. The reply she now takes up is that philosophical language is innocent, and that phrases like blind to the facts are dead idiom carrying no judgment of anybody. She says these phrases use blindness as the ready-made image of failure, and most philosophers concede that much. The argument below is about the ones they want to keep.

The defence she is answering. Philosophers who concede that blind to the implications and morally blind are ableist still want to keep justice is blind and blind review, on the grounds that these associate blindness with something good. Justice is a virtue. So the metaphor is a compliment.

Her answer, in one step. The phrase does not describe how good justice is. It describes how justice works: by not knowing who the parties are. Personal characteristics and circumstances are treated as irrelevant and withheld from the people deciding.

So the association is between blindness and not knowing, which is the thing being praised. Same for blind review, where what is praised is that the reviewer does not know whose paper it is. The word blind is doing one job in that sentence, and the job is standing in for a capacity somebody lacks. That is what it is doing inside the compliment too.

The test she offers. If justice is blind were a statement about how good justice is, then saying “justice is blind, and that is a bad thing” would be a contradiction. It is not a contradiction. “That is a bad thing” means that a justice system which refuses to look at who somebody is, and what their circumstances are, gets things wrong. Legal theorists including Martha Minow and Martha Fineman argue exactly that, and nobody takes them to be contradicting themselves.

Ableist exceptionism, which is her coinage and worth having for an exam. It names the following: philosophers who argue at length that language constructs social reality, and that sexist and racist speech helps produce inequality, then treat disability metaphors as politically neutral figures of speech. The metaphors in question are the ordinary furniture of philosophical writing: blind to the implications, deaf to criticism, tone-deaf, a lame argument, a crippled economy, moral myopia, paralysed by indecision, a crazy view, an idiotic objection, a blind spot.

The exception is to their own rule. These philosophers hold that language builds social reality, so that sexist and racist speech helps produce the inequality it names. They apply that rule to sex and to race, and stop at disability. It would be very strange if linguistic practices built every other social inequality and happened to leave this one alone, so the exemption is a choice somebody is making.

The same shape, in the research. Philosophers have produced a large literature on implicit bias, meaning unconscious attitudes that affect hiring and grading, measured by a test called the Implicit Association Test, and on stereotype threat, the idea that knowing you belong to a stigmatised group worsens your performance. Tremain raises two problems. The test's reliability has been attacked by philosophers of science, and stereotype threat is one of the claims in social psychology's replication crisis. And every one of those studies looks at gender, or race, or the two together. The Rutgers-Syracuse experiment on disabled applicants exists, and the philosophical literature on bias does not cite it.

Fricker's two cases, which Tremain says are the same case

The question When is not having the words for your own situation an injustice, and when is it just bad luck?

Start with Fricker's term. Hermeneutical injustice means being wronged because the shared stock of concepts has no name for something that happens to you, and has none because people like you had no hand in making that stock.

Fricker's case for it. A woman with postpartum depression in the mid twentieth century. She and her husband both treat her state as a personal failing, because there is no other available description. Then she joins a consciousness-raising group and hears, over forty-five minutes, that it is a mix of physiological facts and isolation. Fricker's verdict: she was wronged in her capacity as a knower, because women were shut out of making the concepts.

What the wrong consists of, since it is easy to read it as being about suffering. The woman cannot make her own experience intelligible, to herself or to anybody else. With no word for it, she cannot think about it, describe it, or ask for anything, and the only description on offer is that she is failing as a wife and mother. What was taken from her is the ability to understand her own life.

Fricker's contrasting case, three pages later. A man has a medical condition that affects how he behaves around other people, at a time when that condition has no name and no diagnosis. He cannot explain himself. Other people read him as rude or unstable, and he is harmed by it. Fricker's verdict: no injustice here. A poignant case of circumstantial bad luck.

What Fricker says separates them. In the first case there were background social conditions that produced the gap: women were powerless relative to men, so they had unequal say in making the concepts. In the second case, on her account, there were no such conditions. Nobody made the concept missing. It just was.

Tremain’s objection, which is that the background conditions are there in both The norms that decide what counts as acceptable behaviour, acceptable communication, rationality and emotional self-control are produced, and produced by the apparatus of disability. A person whose behaviour falls outside them is discredited, ignored and stigmatised by a machine, and never by chance. Until the mad pride movement and its relatives, the concepts those people needed in order to see their situation as political were unavailable to them, for the same kind of reason women lacked the concepts for postpartum depression.

So the charge is specific. Fricker has put the disability case in the bad-luck box because she assumes disability is outside power to begin with, which is the assumption the whole book is attacking. And putting it there, in a book about epistemic injustice, is itself an instance of the thing the book is about.

What makes this worth having for an exam. Fricker is the standard reference on epistemic injustice, and this is a published objection to her on her own ground, by somebody who otherwise thinks her work is useful.

Why she refuses the phrase “epistemology of ignorance”

The question Week 6 gave us a term for actively produced not-knowing. Why does Tremain drop it?

The term, and where it comes from. Charles Mills, in The Racial Contract of 1997, writes that the agreement among white people produces an inverted epistemology, an epistemology of ignorance, with the result that white people are in general unable to understand the world they themselves made. Philosophers of race and feminist philosophers built a field on it, and week 6 used it.

Tremain's first objection: the term is ableist. Its users are explicit that they mean laws, schools, newspapers and habits, and that they are never saying any individual has a broken brain. Then look at the words they reach for when they have to describe the thing: obliviousness, delusion, collective amnesia, blindness, moral blindness, cognitive dysfunction. Delusion is a psychiatric diagnosis, amnesia is a memory disorder, and cognitive dysfunction is a clinical phrase. The promise is that this names a social structure, and the vocabulary available for it is the vocabulary of a mind that does not work.

And the sentence she quotes to finish it, from Stacy Clifford Simplican, on what the term implies once you line it up with its opposite: Better politics demands smarter people. If ignorance marks the morally inferior, then the cognitively superior come out morally superior, which is the opposite of what anybody wanted the term for.

Her second objection: it is classist. How much somebody knows depends on literacy, schooling, technology, mobility, food and shelter. An epistemology that scores people on knowledge without counting any of that is scoring them on their circumstances.

Her replacement: the epistemology of domination. Same job, and the name points at the structure.

If you keep one thing. Impairment is both an effect of and a mechanism of the apparatus of disability. That one clause is the difference between Tremain and the British social model, and the British social model is what most of the field holds. Everything else in the chapter follows from it: if there is no neutral biological layer, then philosophy cannot hand disability over to medicine, and the two per cent figure stops being a coincidence.
My noteThe long middle section on the profession reads quickly and is full of numbers, so it is cheap marks. Have the 2 per cent against 22 per cent comparison, the 26 per cent résumé result, and the 24 of 1,552 at Berkeley. The three accounts of disability in 1.3 are the thing an essay question will most likely want, because the whole field turns on them. Ableist exceptionism and epistemology of domination are her two coinages and both are one-line definitions. If a question invites a criticism of a named philosopher, use 1.7 on Fricker.

CH 2Power and Normalization

Shelley L. Tremain, Foucault and Feminist Philosophy of Disability, University of Michigan Press, 2017
Read properly34 pp, printed pages 47 to 80
Where chapter 1 left off. It claimed impairment is produced by the apparatus of disability and gave almost no account of how. This chapter is the how, and it never mentions disability for the first several pages, because the machinery was built for everybody.

What you get out of this chapter. One continuous story, running from the eighteenth-century city to the word on a diagnosis. A state starts managing populations, needs a way of measuring them, gets one from an astronomer, and borrows a word from medicine that says a measurement is also a verdict. From there everything follows: the five steps that sort people, the reason a power devoted to life still kills, the way it governs without force, and the method for taking any of it apart. The Foucault vocabulary arrives inside that story, explained by somebody using it for something. Biopower, discipline, normalization, government, genealogy, dispositif: six terms that turn up across philosophy and that the course has so far used without defining. It also gives you a dated account of where the word normal came from, which is late-eighteenth-century medicine, and one worked case of a reforming movement producing exactly what it set out to end.

Biopower, and the century it came from

The question What changed about government in Europe in the eighteenth century?

The answer: government stopped being mainly a collector of taxes and became a manager of populations. People moved into Western European cities, or were brought there by force under slavery, in numbers that had no precedent. That concentration created new problems for whoever was in charge: sanitation, overcrowding, hunger, infant mortality, the balance between how many people there were and what there was to feed them.

Population is the new object, and Foucault stresses it is a modern idea. Before the eighteenth century there was no such thing to govern.

Biopower is his name for the set of techniques that emerged to manage it. His own definition, from the 1977 lectures: the set of mechanisms through which the basic biological features of the human species became the object of a political strategy. Or, in the shortest form, the moment when Western societies took on board the fact that human beings are a species.

What the old picture of power got wrong, on Foucault's account. From Aristotle through Locke and Rousseau to Rawls, political philosophy asked about legitimacy and sovereignty: what makes rule legitimate, what grounds rights. Those accounts treat power as a thing an individual holds, like a possession, in the form of inherent rights, which can then be handed over to make a sovereign.

His counterclaims, and they are the part to remember.

  1. Power is not held, it is exercised. It exists only in action.
  2. Power is not fundamentally repressive. Violence and consent are instruments or results of it, and neither is what it is.
  3. Power works best through productive constraints. It makes things, including the categories of person it then governs.
  4. So the question to ask is how. By what means is it exercised?

Why sex is at the centre of it. A form of power aimed at the biological features of a species has to take an interest in how the species reproduces. From the eighteenth century the state needed to know about age at marriage, legitimacy of births, frequency of sexual relations, the effect of contraception.

And this is where Foucault contradicts the usual story. The period is normally described as one of repression and silence about sex. His claim is the opposite: an explosion of discourse about sex, because the state needed to know.

What biopower built. Public hygiene medicine with centralised institutions. Campaigns to medicalise the new thing called a population. The idea of a health risk, and from it mass interventions such as immunisation and sewers. Insurance and savings schemes against accident and illness. Censuses and statistics to produce forecasts and averages. And security mechanisms that separate out parts of a population, in Foucault's own list blind people, lepers, witches and perverts, so as to maximise the conditions for life.

Tremain's line about disability's place in this. The methods for watching and classifying disability were not a by-product of biopower. They were instrumental in bringing it into being.

Where the word normal came from, which is medicine

The question A state that sets out to manage a population has to be able to say what is normal for one. Where did that word come from?

The answer: it is a nineteenth-century invention that carries a judgment inside a description. Ian Hacking supplies the history. The sense of normal meaning usual, regular, common or typical becomes current after 1840, with a first citation of normal, or typical in 1828. It came out of the study of life, and specifically out of medicine.

The sequence of ideas, in the late 1700s.

  1. Disease moves into the organ. It stops being a condition of a whole body and becomes an attribute of a particular organ, investigable through the fluids an organ secretes.
  2. The normal is defined as the leftover. A state is normal if it is not associated with a pathological organ. So the normal is secondary to and derived from the pathological.
  3. Then Broussais inverts it. On his principle, disease is irritation of the tissue of an organ, and the pathological becomes defined as deviation from the normal. Variation becomes variation from a normal state, and the pathological stops being different in kind and becomes continuous with the normal.

Why that matters for statistics. Broussais, whom Auguste Comte credits with the modern sense of the normal, was connected with the first use of statistical data to evaluate a medical treatment.

Hacking’s sentence about what the word does The word normal uses a power as old as Aristotle to bridge the gap between fact and value, whispering in your ear that what is normal is also all right. It stands at once for what is typical, which is an unenthusiastic average, and for good health and our chosen destiny. That is why he calls this benign and sterile-sounding word one of the most powerful ideological tools of the twentieth century.

And here is the move Tremain makes with it. If the category of the normal is a historical artefact, then anything whose identity is established by its distance from that category must be a historical artefact too. Disability is defined by departure from the normal. So disability is an artefact, and so is impairment.

That is the argument of the week in two sentences, and it is why chapter 2 exists.

Normalization, and the five things it does

The question Given a word that makes a measurement into a verdict, what can an institution do with it?

The answer: by averaging. The modern idea of normal consolidated alongside the new statistics, and François Ewald's formulation is the useful one: the norm let a power aimed at producing and ordering social strength take over the work that seizure and repression used to do. It turned the negative restraints of law into the positive controls of normalization.

What technologies of normalization do. They isolate what gets called an anomaly in a population so that corrective techniques can act on it. Tremain's warning: they are not neutral responses to anomalies that were already there. They systematically produce the perception of an anomaly in the first place, impairment among them.

Dividing practices, Foucault's term: ways of combining a scientific discourse with segregation, so that an undifferentiated mass of people gets sorted into mad and sane, sick and healthy, criminal and law-abiding. Through them a person becomes attached to an identity and comes to understand themselves in scientific terms.

The five operations, which is the list to memorise. Normalization works as a punishment, and it:

  1. Compares. Every action is referred to a whole that is at once a field of comparison and a rule to follow.
  2. Differentiates. Individuals are separated from each other by their distance from that rule, which functions as a floor, an average or a target.
  3. Hierarchises. Natures, grades, levels and abilities are ranked and given numbers.
  4. Homogenises. The ranking introduces a conformity that has to be reached.
  5. Excludes. The far side of the line, the abnormal, is codified and enforced by a penalty: correction, segregation.

Why it is phrased as a punishment. Foucault found normalization first in eighteenth-century military schools, orphanages and boarding schools, where it worked as a perpetual penalty. The aim is a docile body, meaning one that can be used, transformed and improved.

Discipline enables subjects to act in order to constrain them That sentence is Tremain's summary of modern discipline and it is worth holding, because it is the thing that separates this account of power from the one where power says no. You are given more to do, more finely specified, and the specification is the control.

Discipline and security, and why the distinction matters. Foucault separates two ways normalization runs, and insists the second did not replace the first. Sovereign power, discipline and security all operate at once; what changes is the emphasis.

How it worksWhich comes first
Discipline Encloses a space and trains within it. School, barracks, prison The norm is set, and the normal is derived from it. Foucault suggests calling this normation
Security Expands outward, taking in production, behaviour, markets, consumers The normal is plotted first, from the distributions, and the norm is derived from it. This is normalization in the strict sense

What security does with the curves. It plots the normal and the abnormal together, and then works to bring the less favourable distributions towards the more favourable ones, gradually.

Racism against the abnormal

The question The fifth operation is exclusion. How far will a power devoted to improving life take it?

The answer: on his account they are the same machinery, and the dividing line is normal against abnormal. Foucault's thesis, which Ladelle McWhorter develops, is that modern racism is racism against the abnormal. What is at issue in a modern racist regime is not religion, culture or skin colour as such, but whether a person falls on the normal side.

What that widens it to include. Nonwhite skin and non-Christian affiliation are marked abnormal, and so are low scores on an intelligence test, seizures, cleft palates, intersex, being trans and same-gender coupling. Racism, on this account, is preoccupied with protecting the boundaries of the one race that matters, the human race in its highest representatives.

A case showing the two categories being made together. In 1921 the physician Pearce Bailey told the American Neurological Association that Black and Native American people could not reach the average intelligence of the cultured races, and that the existence of a mental disease implies a developed intelligence. So on his reasoning they suffered less madness because they were inferior. Sander Gilman and James Thomas point out such links were used to claim certain races were suited to certain occupations, sharecropping among them.

Why a power devoted to life needs racism, which is the hardest claim in the chapter. Foucault's argument, and his sentence: if the power of normalization wished to exercise the old sovereign right to kill, it must become racist.

The reasoning in steps. Biopower justifies itself by improving and extending life. A power so justified cannot straightforwardly kill. Racism supplies the connection that lets it: it establishes a relation between my life and another's death, with biology as the reference point, so that the other's death makes life in general healthier and purer.

And killing here is wider than killing. Foucault is explicit that it includes indirect forms: exposing somebody to death, increasing the risk of death for some people, political death, expulsion.

Where evolutionary language comes in. Not Darwin's theory as such, but a bundle of ideas taken from it, the hierarchy of species, the struggle for existence, selection eliminating the less fit, which by the mid nineteenth century had become a way of thinking about colonisation, war, criminality, madness and the classes of a society.

Government, which means the conduct of conduct

The question Almost none of this is done by force. So what is a government actually doing to people?

The answer: acting on what people do, by shaping what they can do. Foucault takes government in a sixteenth-century sense, broader than states: any activity that aims to shape, guide or affect conduct, your own or somebody else's. His definition is the conduct of conduct.

What that pulls into view. Your relation to yourself. Relations with other people that involve guidance or control. Relations inside institutions. And then things nobody had counted as power at all: weight-loss programmes, fitness regimes, assertiveness training, cosmetic surgery, psychotherapy, rehabilitation. Tremain calls these technologies of the self, alongside the technologies of normalization that sort people as deaf, criminal or mad.

Foucault's own formulation, which is the most quoted sentence in the chapter: a relationship of power is a mode of action that does not act directly on others but acts upon their actions. It incites, induces, seduces, makes easier or more difficult, and at the limit constrains or forbids. It is always a way of acting on a subject who is capable of acting.

Why liberalism is the setting for all of this. Foucault treats liberalism as an art of government, where others call it an ideology: a way of rationalising government based on legal subjects who have rights and freedoms. Because its subjects are held to have those freedoms, liberalism can always criticise and remake itself, which is why it survives its own critics.

The trick that makes it efficient. A power that manages people through their freedom costs the state less than one that coerces them. Wendy Brown's line: liberal government produced, organised, managed and consumed individual freedom, all without touching the subject.

And where it went after the 1950s. Neoliberalism, which Brown describes as the whole social field being turned into an economy, and subjects being made responsible for their own outcomes within it. Management at a distance, through norms and standards that people freely try to meet.

Totalizing and individualizing, which happen together

The question A government managing everybody also keeps inventing finer labels for each person. Why do those two go together?

The answer: those are one process, and this is the chapter's most useful idea for the course. Foucault's phrase is omnes et singulatim, of all and of each. To be an individual in the modern sense is to be attached to a totality.

How the two feed each other. The state's reach over everybody grows exactly as its descriptions of each person get finer. Distinguishing people, ranking them, classifying them against a set of normalising criteria makes them into recognisable kinds, and recognisable kinds are interchangeable, which is what makes a population governable.

Which is how to read the growing list of kinds of disability Physical against cognitive. Severe against moderate. Attention deficit disorder against attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. Tremain reads each new distinction as a product of biopower doing both things at once: a finer description of an individual, and a longer reach for the machine that describes.

Foucault on the individual, in the sentence she quotes. The individual is no doubt a fictitious atom of an ideological representation of society; but he is also a reality fabricated by this specific technology of power that I have called discipline.

The Normalization Movement, and what it did to sex

The question Everything above is machinery. Here is one movement running on it: can a campaign led by the people it is for still enforce the norm it set out to break?

What the movement was. In the last decades of the twentieth century, Wolf Wolfensberger built a social movement on a normalization principle that denounced the forced institutionalisation of cognitively impaired people. It was made largely of those people and their allies, and it wanted them out of institutions and living in communities. Michael Oliver's summary of the principle: normalization theory offers disabled people the chance to be given valued social roles in an unequal society that values some roles more than others.

The renaming, and why it did not fix anything. The principle was later called social role valorization, to stress that normalization is about what gets valued. Chris Drinkwater's objection is that the renaming left untouched the power that decides what a normal life is. The assumption underneath stayed: that these people should learn valued behaviour in order to get a valued life.

The case, from Lindsay Williams and Melanie Nind. Leaving institutions raised a question for the movement's leaders about sex, since people who had been institutionalised for years were assumed to be inexperienced. So the movement produced sex-education texts through the 1970s.

What those texts did well. They were a real step in self-advocacy. They set out how this group had been denied sexual self-determination, and the part forced sterilisation and gender segregation had played in it.

And what they also did.

  • They assumed love, sex and fulfilment would and should come together, in that order.
  • They left out same-gender love and sex completely, into the 1990s.
  • Some of them described same-gender relationships as deviant and inappropriate, which is to say, as not among the valued roles.
  • Some distinguished between true homosexuals and cognitively impaired people who, these authors said, had turned to same-gender sex only because normal heterosexual sex was unavailable to them.

Tremain's verdict. The literature reproduced the apparatuses of gender and heterosexism while expanding the apparatus of disability, and it is an exemplary product of modern power for exactly the reason given above: it was enabling and constraining at the same time.

The implantation thesis, which generalises the point. Foucault argued that sexual perversions were not conditions medicine discovered. They are artefacts of expert discourse, implanted in the people said to have them, and the more psychiatrists looked for deviants the more they found.

Barry Allen's extension to disability, which is the sentence to take: there can be no notion of impairment without reference to a statistically normal case, just as there can be no criminal without a law. A discourse about biomedical norms, however scientific, is no more true to nature than a discourse about criminals. Any norm is an artefact of the discipline that measures it.

The method underneath it, and the reason for doing any of this

The question If every category in this chapter was made by somebody, what is the point of saying so?

Start with what a discourse is, because the word has been doing work for eight sections and it does not mean language. Tremain says philosophers and disability theorists get this wrong, taking it for communication. A discourse is the whole set of conditions that decide what can count as a statement at all: what gets said, how, in which settings, by whom, whose saying it carries authority, and what historical conditions made the topic sayable.

Karen Barad's version, which is the clearest. Discourses are not signifying systems or grammars or speech acts. They are the historically specific material conditions that enable and constrain what can be known, and they are actual social conditions, with nothing transcendental about them.

So discursive practices produce the subjects and objects of knowledge, where you might have thought they only describe them. The average man, the abnormal, the impaired body and the delinquent all came into existence inside a discourse that had the authority to say so.

Nominalism, which is the metaphysics underneath. The world does not arrive divided into categories waiting to be discovered. People do the dividing, construct facts, and then verify statements about them. There are no natural kinds and no natural order.

Foucault's own statement of it, from 1972: what he wants is to dispense with things, and to put in place of the supposed treasure of things existing before discourse the regular formation of objects that emerge only within it.

What she does and does not claim. She sets aside stars, photons and horses, since whether those have identities before anyone names them is not her fight. Her only commitments about what exists are commitments about human history and practice.

Genealogy, since it is the method behind all of this. Foucault took the word from Nietzsche and called his own versions histories of the present. A genealogy asks how our current ways of thinking came to be possible, and refuses to ask why we think as we do, because a why-question takes the thinking subject as given and starts from there.

What a genealogy is for, in his words: the insurrection of knowledges. It digs up subjugated knowledges, meaning ones ruled inadequate or insufficiently scientific, such as the knowledge of the psychiatrised person, the delinquent, the nurse, so that what we take as self-evident can be seen as contingent.

And the sentence that states the point of the whole method, from Foucault: that which exists did not always exist, things that give us the impression of being the most obvious were formed through a fragile and precarious history, and since these things were made, they can, provided one knows how they were made, be unmade.

If you keep one thing. Normal is a word from the 1820s that came out of medicine, and it works by smuggling a judgment into a description: what is typical, and therefore all right. Everything else in this chapter is machinery for producing and enforcing that word. Say that, and you can derive the rest.
My noteThis chapter is one argument with a vocabulary attached, so revise it forwards: the city, the average man, the word, the five steps, the exclusion, the gentle government. The terms stick when they hang off that sequence. The four terms worth being able to define cold are biopower, normalization and its five operations, government as the conduct of conduct, and genealogy. The discipline against security table is the kind of distinction an exam likes, because it has a clean one-line difference: discipline sets the norm first, security reads the normal off the distribution first. The Normalization Movement in 2.7 is the only worked case in the chapter, so it is the example to use for any question about reform reproducing what it opposes.
Where it belongs in the quarrel
  • It supplies what chapter 1 assumed. Chapter 1 says disability is an apparatus. This says what an apparatus is and where this one came from.
  • It answers week 3. Kuhn said science runs on shared commitments. Foucault's styles of reasoning, taken from Ian Hacking here, make the stronger claim that a style brings into being the very sentences that can be true or false within it.
  • It is answered by anybody who wants a politics. If the subject is an effect of power all the way down, critics ask what is left to do the resisting. Tremain says chapter 3 answers this, which is next week's reading.

Glossary

apparatus, dispositif
Foucault's term, and the one the book is built on. A mixed, interconnected collection of discourses, institutions, buildings, regulations, laws, scientific statements, administrative measures and moral propositions, which comes together in response to an urgent need at a particular moment. The apparatus of disability is one; so, on this account, are race and sex.
impairment
On the British social model, a neutral fact about a body on which disability is then imposed. On Tremain's account there is no such neutral fact: impairment is both an effect of and a mechanism of the apparatus of disability.
the British social model, BSM
From the United Kingdom in the 1970s. Impairment is a neutral characteristic; disability is the social oppression imposed on people who have one. Built on a parallel with sex and gender, and grounded in historical materialism, so its politics is systemic.
the individualist or medical account
Disability is a functional limitation of a particular person, answered by accommodating that person into an environment that stays as it was. Tremain's objection is that it keeps the eugenic push of the medical model it claims to replace.
biopower
The set of mechanisms through which the biological features of the human species became the object of political strategy, from the eighteenth century. Its object is the population, which is itself a modern idea.
population
A group of living beings treated as a single thing to be managed, with its own birth rates, death rates and risks. Foucault's point is that it is an idea that did not exist before the eighteenth century.
normalization
The work of isolating anomalies in a population so that corrective techniques can act on them. Five operations: comparison, differentiation, hierarchy, homogeneity, exclusion.
normation
Foucault's suggested name for how discipline works, as against normalization proper. Under discipline the norm is set first and the normal is derived from it. Under security the normal is plotted first, from the distributions, and the norm derived from that.
discipline
A type of power and a way of exercising it, which encloses a space and trains within it: schools, barracks, hospitals, prisons. Its aim is a docile body, one that can be used, transformed and improved. Tremain's summary: discipline enables subjects to act in order to constrain them.
security
The later emphasis, which expands outward where discipline encloses, taking in production, behaviour and markets, and works by shifting distributions gradually. It did not replace discipline; the three forms, sovereignty, discipline and security, run at once.
dividing practices
Modes of manipulation that combine a scientific discourse with segregation, sorting an undifferentiated mass of people into mad and sane, sick and healthy, criminal and law-abiding, so that a person becomes tied to an identity.
government, the conduct of conduct
Foucault's broad sixteenth-century sense: any activity that aims to shape, guide or affect conduct, one's own or anybody else's. It brings weight-loss programmes and psychotherapy into the analysis of power alongside prisons.
governmentality
A rationality of government: a system of thinking about the practice of governing that makes it thinkable and acceptable both to those who do it and those it is done to.
technologies of the self
Techniques by which a person acts on themselves: fitness regimes, assertiveness training, cosmetic surgery, rehabilitation. On this account they belong to the analysis of power, since power works by guiding conduct.
genealogy
Foucault's method, taken from Nietzsche, which he also called a history of the present. It asks how our current ways of thinking became possible, and refuses the question of why we think as we do, because that takes the thinking subject as given.
subjugated knowledges
Knowledges disqualified as inadequate or insufficiently scientific, low on the hierarchy: the knowledge of the psychiatrised person, the delinquent, the nurse. Genealogy digs them up.
styles of reasoning
Ian Hacking's idea, which Tremain extends with her own diagnostic style. A style brings into being new kinds of objects and new candidates for truth and falsehood, so a sentence of that kind can only be true or false within the style. Styles are self-authenticating, which is why they are stable.
implantation thesis
Foucault on perversions: they were not conditions medicine discovered but artefacts of expert discourse, implanted in the people said to have them. Barry Allen extends it to impairment, since there is no impairment without a statistically normal case, as there is no criminal without a law.
nominalism
The position that the world does not arrive divided into categories waiting to be discovered. There are no natural kinds and no natural order; people classify, construct facts and then verify statements about them.
ableist exceptionism
Tremain's coinage. The phenomenon of disability being singled out for exemption: a theorist argues that language constructs social reality and produces inequality, and then treats disability metaphors as neutral figures of speech.
epistemology of ignorance
Charles Mills's term, from The Racial Contract: an inverted epistemology produced by white supremacy, with the result that white people cannot understand the world they made. Week 6 used it.
epistemology of domination
Tremain's replacement for it, on the grounds that the first is ableist, since it cannot be described without reaching for cognitive failure, and classist, since how much a person knows depends on literacy, schooling and money.
hermeneutical injustice
Miranda Fricker's term: being wronged because the shared stock of concepts has no name for something that happens to you, and has none because people like you had no hand in making it.
racism against the abnormal
Foucault's thesis, developed by Ladelle McWhorter. What is at issue in a modern racist regime is not religion, culture or skin colour as such but whether a person falls on the normal side of the line, which is why the same machinery marks seizures, intersex and same-gender coupling.
omnes et singulatim
Of all and of each. Foucault's name for the double movement by which modern states totalise and individualise at once: the finer the description of each person, the longer the reach over everybody.
old and new biosocial science
Dorothy Roberts's pair. The old says biological difference produces social inequality, so fix the bodies. The new says social inequality produces biological difference, so there is no natural body.
accommodationism
The policy by which one group's requirements are built into a place as standard, and everybody else makes a documented, medically certified request for the equivalent. Tremain's objection is the unequal effort, and never the provision itself.
