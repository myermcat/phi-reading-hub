CH 4Stratification and Discrimination

Sergio Sismondo, An Introduction to Science and Technology Studies, 2nd ed., Wiley-Blackwell, 2010
Read properly11 pp, pages 36 to 46

Does science reward the best work, or does work become the best by being rewarded?

The number the chapter opens on

Of the 55 Nobel laureates working in the United States in 1963, 34 had studied or collaborated with one of 46 previous prize-winners. And the ones who had worked with a laureate before doing their own important research collected their prizes at an average age of 44, against 53 for everyone else.

Then the question the whole chapter is built on, in Sismondo's own framing. Are those clusters the tip of a merit-based iceberg, or artefacts of a prestige system gone wrong? Is the knowledge elites are recognised for intrinsically valuable, or does it become valuable because of its association with elites?

Two predictions on the record, both wrong. Francis Bacon, in the week 2 packet, thought his inductive method would level the differences between intellects and let science be industrialised: anyone following the procedure would do useful work. Three hundred years later the Spanish philosopher José Ortega y Gasset claimed science had progressed thanks in great part to the work of men astoundingly mediocre, and even less than mediocre.

The distributions say otherwise. Roughly 10 per cent of scientific authors produce 50 per cent of all papers. An estimated 80 per cent of citations go to 20 per cent of papers. Science is not flat and it is not a machine that runs on interchangeable operators. Bacon and Ortega are both wrong about its structure.

A warning about the measuring instrument, which is Box 4.1

Citation counts are the evidence for everything above, so the chapter stops to ask whether they measure anything. The assumption is that a citation records an influence: this paper gave me important background, so I cite it.

Sismondo says that is an idealisation, and the reason is what a scientific paper actually is. A paper that looks like an account of an experiment is a rational reconstruction of one. Details are cleaned up, idiosyncrasies are left out, and the order of events is changed to make the right narrative. The paper is an argument.

From which it follows that its citations are vehicles for the argument. They are chosen for what will persuade the intended audience. Informal conversation, the thing somebody told you at a conference, the paper too obvious to cite, all of it is invisible. Citations can also do several jobs at once. His conclusion is flat: citation analysis is a poor tool for studying communication and influence.

He then uses the numbers anyway, on the grounds that they are so lopsided that even a crude measure carries the point. Which is a move worth noticing, because it is the shape of a lot of arguments in this field: the instrument is bad, the signal is big, and the author carries on with the caveat stated.

The same numbers, two opposite policies

This is the most useful three paragraphs in the chapter, because it shows a fact doing no work on its own.

Jonathan and Stephen Cole read the figures as efficiency. If 80 per cent of citations go to 20 per cent of papers, put the funding where the influential work is. Science is already stratified; funding should reflect that. Most of the less-cited papers need not have been written, and given how often the same discovery is made independently, the work would have happened anyway.

Stephen Turner and Daryl Chubin read the same figures as waste. The Coles' conclusion rests on an assumption that the top 20 per cent are cited because they are intrinsically more valuable. Deny that, and the numbers say science is ignoring a majority of perfectly good work. The policy then goes the other way: level the playing field, reduce the effects of prestige and old-boy networks.

What the disagreement turns on, in Sismondo's words, is how accurate science's system of recognition is. And that question is difficult to answer, which is why the same table supports both policies.

Example · and a third objection nobody in that argument raised

Both camps assume the publication is where scientific value lives. Chandra Mukerji suggests oceanography gets government support because it maintains a pool of skilled oceanographers who can be called on when needed. On that account the papers are almost a by-product and the standing capability is the thing being funded. Elsewhere, skills and knowledge move into the world directly, as laboratory tools and products, without passing through a journal at all. Sismondo does not think either alternative is right in general. He raises them to show what a narrow focus on publications leaves out.

What makes a scientist productive, and why the lists are useless

No single variable dominates. One survey builds a portrait of the most productive scientist as someone who is driven, has a strong ego, acts autonomously, plays with ideas, tolerates ambiguity, is at home with abstractions, is detached from non-scientific social relations, reserves mornings for writing, works on routinised problems, is relatively young, went to a prestigious graduate school, had a prestigious mentor, is at a prestigious employer, has freedom to choose problems, has a history of success, and has been cited before.

A later study offers shorter adjectives, in order of importance: persistent, smart, civil, creative, entrepreneurial, aggressive, tasteful, confident, adaptable.

Sismondo's verdict on both lists is worth copying. Many of the items are uncontroversial and none of them is particularly helpful to somebody trying to understand scientific processes. Peak productive years cluster around the mid-forties, and nobody knows why or what would change it. Persistence may matter enormously, and the sources of persistence are unclear. A list of correlates is not an explanation.

One variable does something, though, and it is the one that matters. Scientists at prestigious institutions are strikingly more productive, and institutional prestige is a strong predictor.

The obvious explanation is selection, and the evidence rules it out. Do prestigious departments simply hire people who were going to be more productive anyway? Not in general: the differences in productivity emerge after people have been hired. And it is hard to imagine any hiring committee picking that consistently, in a job market that is almost always tight, between candidates whose potential differs very slightly.

So prestige is doing something. Better facilities. Better networks. More intellectual stimulation. More internal pressure and reward. Or simply more visibility, and therefore more reward for the same work, which Sismondo states as a conclusion with an exclamation mark: if that is right, then prestige by itself makes people's research valuable.

The Matthew Effect, which is the chapter's engine

Robert Merton named it after a line in the Gospel of Matthew: to whoever has, more shall be given, and from whoever has not, even what he has shall be taken away.

The mechanism is a chain where each link is small and the compounding is not. Somebody with the traits on those lists is more likely to get a famous mentor. A famous mentor makes a prestigious graduate school more likely. That makes a prestigious department more likely. That brings better facilities, better conversation, and above all more visibility. More visibility means more citations. And citation is itself a reward: people whose work is cited go on publishing. The sociologist Randall Collins calls citations sources of emotional energy.

Discrimination, and the numbers he puts behind it

His framing is that women are worse represented the more elite the grouping, and the figures step down accordingly.

  • United States, 2005. Women earned 46 per cent of doctorates in the social sciences, natural sciences and engineering, up from 31 per cent a decade before. But 49 per cent in the biological sciences and 18 per cent in engineering.
  • United States, 2006. Only 19 per cent of senior faculty in those areas were women, heavily concentrated in the life and social sciences.
  • Europe. Only 15 per cent of senior university faculty across all fields. And the variation between countries is enormous: in 2003 women earned 72 per cent of life science and 45 per cent of physical science doctorates in Italy, against 47 and 23 per cent in Germany. Senior medical faculty: 22 per cent women in the UK, 6 per cent in Germany.
  • Japan stands out among large research nations as particularly gendered, with smaller percentages of women researchers of every kind than the United States or any European country.
  • And in most countries women are unlikely to be gatekeepers: editors of major journals, chairs of important boards.

The country variation is the part to think about. Italy and Germany are both rich European countries with good universities, and the gap between 72 and 47 per cent has nothing to do with biology. Whatever is producing these numbers travels with the culture.

Box 4.2: the golden age that was a dark age

Margaret Rossiter's history of women scientists in America turns on one finding, and it is counter-intuitive: feminisation is linked to a fall in status, and masculinisation to a rise in it. The direction of causation is from who holds the jobs to how the jobs are regarded.

What that produced, twice. When women began entering scientific fields in the 1860s and 1870s, the men already there perceived that their work was losing its masculine status, and pushed women into marginal positions where they would be invisible. In the twentieth century women found they could enter much more easily through new fields, home economics and nutritional science, where they could do sociology, economics, biology and chemistry under another name. The fields as a whole were then poorly regarded, because they were feminised.

And the sentence in the box that people quote. Women's colleges had been a safe place for women scientists to work and build credentials. After the Second World War, as American universities expanded, those colleges and the less prestigious universities followed the leading ones in hiring more men. The golden age of American science after the war was a dark age for women in it.

What changed it was not science. The shift came in the 1960s, from outside: the National Organization for Women, Betty Friedan, and Alice Rossi's 1965 article in Science titled "Women in Science: Why So Few?", which made the case that science was no different from any other profession. Alongside that ran a legal revolution producing anti-discrimination law and affirmative action, which women then used as resources to get in.

The pipeline, and why Sismondo half rejects it

The standard metaphor is Natalie Angier's. Women flow through a pipe with leaks at every joint along its span, a pipe that begins with a high-pressure surge of young women at the source, a roiling Amazon of smart graduate students, and ends at the spigot with a trickle of women prominent enough to be deans or department heads or to win a Nobel Prize.

His objection is that the picture makes the problem one of leakage, which puts all the attention at the joints and none at either end. The metaphor only works if you also attend to intake, blockage and filters: processes of inclusion and exclusion. In many fields there is no roiling Amazon at the source to begin with, and the women who are there do not simply leak out, they are discouraged the whole way along.

The framing he prefers is Sara Delamont's three problems, and they are the three sub-headings of the rest of the chapter: getting in, staying in, and getting on.

Getting in

Science and engineering have masculine images and the stereotypical scientist is male, and that shapes what girls believe about their own capacity. Quite young girls may find it hard to think of themselves as potential scientists, and in many environments they are actively discouraged by teachers, peers and parents. The consequence is structural and not merely attitudinal: by the end of secondary school many girls who might have been interested already lack the background subjects they would need to study science at university. The door closes before anybody applies.

Example · what the advertisements in Science are teaching

An analysis of the advertising in the journal Science found, in its own words, more cultural content between the covers than we would care to acknowledge. Men are more likely to be depicted as scientific heroes. They are also more likely to be shown as nerds or nonconformists, and more likely to be shown at play. The words simple and easy go with pictures of women; fast, reliable and accurate go with pictures of men. And a female figure can stand as a symbol of nature in a way a male figure cannot, which puts women on the side of the thing being investigated.

Staying in

The concept to take from this section is the unofficial PhD programme. Alongside the official courses, exams and research there runs a second curriculum: study groups, pick-up basketball, informal mentoring, and simply the ongoing conversation and consultation of a department. Even a well-integrated student can be shut out of parts of it.

We would all go to parties together and go and have beer on Friday, but if somebody came in to ask what drying agent to use to clean up THF, they would never ask me. It just wasn't something that would cross their minds.
A woman doctoral student, quoted on page 43. Note what is and is not happening: she is invited to everything social and consulted about nothing technical.

Then the hardest number in the chapter. A study of peer review scores for postdoctoral fellowships at the Swedish Medical Research Council found reviewers rating men's accomplishments as overwhelmingly more valuable than apparently equivalent accomplishments by women. To be given the same competence score as a male colleague, a woman needed to exceed his scientific productivity by about three extra papers in Nature or Science, or twenty extra papers in an excellent specialist journal.

And a clash of clocks. Shirley Tilghman, a molecular biologist and later president of Princeton, argues that the pressure at the start of a career falls hardest on women. Research productivity decides whether a senior graduate student or postdoc gets a permanent job, and new faculty then have about six years to clear an all-or-nothing tenure hurdle. Those are exactly the years in which women are most likely to want to have children.

Getting on

The discriminations at this stage are individually small enough to argue about. Women hired at the same time as male peers tend to have smaller laboratories, and to have negotiated worse arrangements for start-up grants, for the overhead the university takes from their research money, for salary and for teaching load.

And they remain outside the informal networks. Inside their departments they tend not to have informal mentors to steer them through opaque processes. Outside them they do less collaborative research. And they are less well connected to the networks that carry unpublished work, which leaves them more dependent on the published literature and therefore always slightly behind. Sismondo quotes Jonathan Cole's formulation: women have moved into science, but they are not of the community of science.

Which is where the Matthew Effect returns and does the argument's real work. In any individual case these differences look trivial, and a woman may well shrug them off. But if competition is strong enough, and if advantage compounds, the small irritants are enough on their own to keep her from getting on. No single act of discrimination has to be large.

The maths gene, and what he says about it

One side claims a biological propensity. Claims of a maths gene or something like it go back to 1980, and as recently as 2005 the president of Harvard, Lawrence Summers, invoked biology to explain differences in men's and women's success in science and engineering.

Sismondo's reply is two lines and both are worth having. First, genes only act in environments, so a claim that a difference is genetic, stated without the environment, is an ideological statement wearing the clothes of a finding. Second, the claim runs against the trend: boys' and girls' test scores in mathematics have been converging.

The other side claims a difference in style, and he reports it without endorsing it. A number of feminists and non-feminists have argued that women tend to think in more concrete terms, are better served by problem-centred learning, think more relationally, and produce more holistic pictures. He parks the whole question for chapter 7, which is the feminist epistemology material and which is weeks 5 to 7 of this course.

The conclusion, and the question it leaves open

What cumulative advantage buys the argument is a mechanism, a way of explaining how something as vague as a culture produces effects as concrete as a salary. No single obstacle decisively keeps women out of the pipeline or ejects them from it. The compounded effect of all of them may be enough. And it explains why individual women's accounts of succeeding and not succeeding can sound completely different from one another and still fit a pattern.

Then the sentence the chapter title has been leading to. With discrimination in view, science is hard to see as efficiently using its resources. It looks less like an efficient meritocracy and more like an inefficient old boys' network, in which people and ideas become important partly because of where they sit in a social network.

And the claim that connects this chapter to the next one. Elite groups are socially constructed. They are not reflections of a natural hierarchy. He concedes immediately that elites do produce more and better knowledge, because they have the advantages and the reinforcement. The open question is the one he started with and does not close: does some of the knowledge elites are recognised for become important because of its association with them, and could science be significantly different if it were more egalitarian?

The verse is Matthew 13:12, and Merton knew exactly what he was doing

The line is from the parable of the sower: to whoever has, more will be given, and from whoever has not, even what he has will be taken away. In the gospel it is about receptivity to a teaching. Merton borrowed it in 1968 for a paper about how scientific credit accumulates, and the borrowing is sardonic: a verse about spiritual reward describing a funding system.

His wife, the sociologist Harriet Zuckerman, did the underlying research on Nobel laureates, which is the study this chapter opens with. Merton acknowledged that her work was so central that the paper should have been co-authored, and said so publicly, years later.

The Harvard computers were paid by the star

Edward Pickering hired women at the Harvard College Observatory from the 1880s because they could be paid 25 to 50 cents an hour, roughly half a man's wage, and because the work of measuring and cataloguing photographic plates was considered clerical. Around eighty women worked there over four decades.

What they produced was not clerical. Annie Jump Cannon classified some 350,000 stars and devised the spectral scheme, O B A F G K M, that astronomy still uses. Henrietta Swan Leavitt discovered that a certain kind of variable star pulses at a rate fixed by its brightness, which gave astronomy its first reliable way of measuring distance beyond our own galaxy. Hubble used her relation to show the universe is expanding, and said she deserved a Nobel Prize. She had died four years before he could nominate her, and the prize is not awarded posthumously.

The Summers speech cost him the job

In January 2005 Lawrence Summers, then president of Harvard, spoke at a conference on diversifying the science workforce and offered three explanations for why women are under-represented at the top of science: that they are less willing to work eighty-hour weeks, that there is a difference in the distribution of innate aptitude at the high end, and discrimination, which he ranked last.

One attendee walked out. The transcript was released a month later. The Harvard Faculty of Arts and Sciences passed a motion of no confidence in March, the first in the university's history, and he resigned in February 2006. He later pledged 50 million dollars of Harvard money to diversity efforts. His successor was Drew Gilpin Faust, the first woman to lead Harvard in its then 371 years.

Jane Dee ran an experimental household

Box 4.3 of the chapter is about women just outside the formal borders of science. Its example is Jane Dee, wife of the Elizabethan mathematician and alchemist John Dee, who kept the largest library in England at their house in Mortlake and a laboratory alongside it.

Natural philosophy in that period was done at home, which made the household a laboratory and the wife its manager. Jane Dee had to run an establishment full of apparatus, assistants and visitors, act as a full partner in the work, and at the same time occupy a position Elizabethan society had no name for. The point of studying her is not to recover a hidden discovery. It is that the boundary between the household and the laboratory is where the line around science was drawn, and somebody was standing on it.

CH 6The Social Construction of Scientific and Technical Realities

Sergio Sismondo, An Introduction to Science and Technology Studies, 2nd ed., Wiley-Blackwell, 2010
Read properly15 pp, pages 57 to 71

When somebody says a scientific fact is socially constructed, which of seven quite different things are they saying?

What the chapter is doing, which is not what you expect

The phrase arrived in the field in the late 1970s and then went everywhere. Books have claimed to show the construction of facts, of knowledge, of theories, of phenomena, of science, of technologies, of societies. It became the label holding a lot of different work together, and the target of fierce attacks from historians, philosophers and sociologists, usually fighting under the banner of realism.

Sismondo's move is to refuse to defend or attack it, and to take it apart instead. Ian Hacking, writing on constructivism in 1999, had gone the other way: he built the strongest possible version to set against realism, defined by three features, the contingency of facts, nominalism about kinds, and external explanations for why beliefs stay put. On Hacking's version a constructivist says of an established theory that it was not the only one that could have won, that its categories are human impositions and never natural kinds, and that the reasons it succeeded were never evidential reasons.

Sismondo pulls the phrase into seven separate claims instead. Some are plainly anti-realist. Some are not. Several are so obviously true that a realist has to accept them. That is what the chapter is for, and it is why she set it.

What all seven have in common is three reminders: that science and technology are social; that they are active, which is what the building metaphor is for; and that they do not run in a straight line from nature to ideas about nature, so their products are not themselves natural.

Context: what realism is, since it is the other team

Realism, in Sismondo's description, is an intuition and never quite a doctrine: that truths depend more on the natural world than on the people who state them, that there is a way the world is, and that it can be discovered and represented reasonably accurately.

He then points out how loose that is. Realists disagree about what science realistically represents, about what representing accurately means, and about whether the issue is fundamentally about knowledge or about things. And there is no good account of what makes real things real. So realism is usually a negative position, defined against whichever anti-realism is in front of it, and seldom a thesis of its own.

1. The construction of social reality

Where the phrase came from. STS took it from Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann's The Social Construction of Reality of 1966, a book about the sociology of knowledge. Their subject is social reality: the institutions and structures that exist because enough people behave as though they do.

Their conditions are worth having exactly, because they are a recipe. To construct an X you need only three things. That knowledge of X encourages behaviour which makes other people more or less likely to act as though X exists. That knowledge of X is reasonably common. And that knowledge of X gets passed on. Given those, X cannot be wished away, and so it exists.

Example · gender, which is his own

Gender is real, because it is difficult not to take account of it. Gender structures create constraints and resources that people have to reckon with, and treating people as gendered tends to produce gendered people. Then the line that does the philosophical work: genders have causal powers, which is probably the best sign of reality we have. And at the same time they are plainly not simply given by nature, which historical research and the differences between contemporary cultures both show.

So this is a case where socially constructed and real are not opposites. That is the point of putting this sense first.

What it means for science. Knowledge, methods, epistemologies, disciplinary boundaries and styles of work are all real social objects of this kind, and contingently real. Ludwik Fleck made the point in 1935: a scientist who fails to account for a taken-for-granted fact meets resistance from colleagues, and that resistance is the fact's social reality showing itself.

And the sentence that stops this being a criticism. The social reality of knowledge is a precondition of progress. If nothing is reasonably solid, there is nothing to build on.

The active version. Latour and Woolgar tracked one statement about a hormone through the literature as it moved from near-nonsense, to possible, to false, to possibly true, to a solid fact, and catalogued what can be done to a paper along the way: ignore it, cite it approvingly, cite it dismissively, question it strongly or weakly, and finally ignore it again because everyone accepts it. Facts are constructed by scientists, one operation at a time, and never by science in the abstract.

Box 6.1, which is the most memorable thing in the chapter

The general problem: it is hard to say when a discovery happened. Was oxygen discovered when Priestley made a pure sample of it, when Lavoisier explained what it was, or later still when somebody gave an account that matches ours? Augustine Brannigan's answer is an attributional model: discoveries are not events in themselves, they are events retrospectively recognised as origins.

How he became the discoverer, which is a story about a priority dispute and hardly at all about peas. Hugo de Vries had read Mendel before 1900, and the first of his three papers on his own results does not mention him. Carl Correns read de Vries's first paper, realised he had lost the race, and quickly published his own work labelling the finding "Mendel's Law". Having lost priority to a living rival, he assigned it to a dead man instead. De Vries's later papers accept Mendel's priority in awkward afterthoughts and grumble about the obscurity of the journal.

And Sismondo's line. Had Correns read one or two generations further back, the title could have gone to any of several people. Mendel discovered his laws in 1866, but only as a result of the events of 1900.

2. The construction of things and phenomena

This is the sense he calls the most novel, and its subject is objects. Many of the things scientists study are not natural things.

Karin Knorr Cetina's formulation: nature is not to be found in the laboratory. To an outside observer the laboratory shows itself as a place from which nature has been excluded as far as possible.

The evidence is ordinary and that is the force of it. The materials arrive already prepared: chemists buy purified reagents, geneticists use established DNA libraries, biologists use engineered animal models. Once inside, the objects are put in artificial situations to see how they react, and subjected to what Latour calls trials of strength. And at best scientists create phenomena, new stable objects of study that did not previously occur. Hacking's version: most of the phenomena of modern physics are manufactured.

So laboratory knowledge is knowledge about distinctly non-natural things, built by hands-on material work.

And the asymmetry he points out is worth keeping. For technology none of this is the least bit surprising. Technology is expected to give new shape to old materials and leave nature behind. The claim only sounds radical when it is made about science, because science is presumed to display the forms of nature exactly as they are.

3. The construction of material and social environments

The easiest of the seven: science and technology change the world, and changing the world is building it.

Technology, intended and not. The success of the petrol car helped create the suburb and the suburban way of living, and to the extent that manufacturers worked to grow the suburban market, that was deliberate. Computers, programs and networks are designed with their social effects in view: dispersed work, control at a distance, or straight military power.

Science too, and his example is pointed. Research into the causes of gender difference has the effect of naturalising those differences. There is great public appetite for it, so work on genes, hormones and brain differences between men and women is well reported, and more often than not reported to emphasise how inevitable stereotypical behaviour is. There is good reason to expect that the reporting has effects on gender itself, which loops straight back to sense 1.

And policy. Government action is increasingly held accountable to scientific evidence, to the point where almost nothing in health, the economy, the environment or defence can be done unless it can be claimed that a study supports it. Studies shape policies, and policies shape the material and social world.

4. The construction of theories

The plainest sense: scientists build accounts, models and theories on top of data. Science is constructive in a geometrical sense, making a pattern appear given the fixed points that practice has produced.

And the surprise is who else says this. That science constructs representations on top of data is roughly the central claim of logical positivism, which is the Vienna Circle in week 2's packet. For positivism there is an essential contingency in theories: for any good theory you can build others that account for the data equally well. Bas van Fraassen puts it directly: he uses the adjective constructive to indicate his view that scientific activity is construction and never discovery, construction of models that must be adequate to the phenomena, and not discovery of truth about the unobservable.

Where the positivists get off the train is that they thought this contingency was largely eliminated by prior decisions about frameworks: scientists settle the logical framework first, then work inside it.

Sismondo says it cannot be that tidy. Given the problem of induction, there is no mechanical route from a finite pile of data to a good theory. When Knorr Cetina explains why particular theories succeed she points to established practices, earlier decisions, concepts stretched to new uses, tinkering and local contingencies. Representations of nature are connected to nature and do not necessarily correspond to it in any strong sense.

Example · two disputes settled by something other than the evidence

Newton against Leibniz. The choice between their metaphysics may have been bound up with political circumstances, which is Steven Shapin's argument.

Pasteur against Pouchet on spontaneous generation. The particular way that dispute was resolved was shaped by the composition of the prize committee that decided it. Pasteur is remembered as having settled the question experimentally. The historical work says the committee mattered too.

Sismondo's reason for using controversies is structural: a controversy is a moment when the alternatives are visible, because both are still being argued for, and it also shows you the forces that close the argument down.

Context: Box 6.2, realism against empiricism

The classic empiricist argument against believing theories. All the evidence for a theory is empirical data. Two theories making the same predictions cannot be told apart by data. Any theory is one of infinitely many empirically equivalent theories. So there is no reason to think yours is the true one, and truth in the usual sense is superfluous.

The realist replies. Data is not the only evidence: it is desirable that a theory be consistent with metaphysical commitments, which cuts the field down, so the infinity of rivals is an illusion because only a few are plausible. And there is no way to make sense of science's success without the approximate truth of its best theories.

Richard Boyd's version is the sharpest. Do not argue about how well theories predict. Argue about how well background theories shape research, because the truth of those is the best explanation of why scientific methods reliably work at all.

5. Heterogeneous construction

The term to learn here is heterogeneous engineering, from John Law. An entrepreneurial engineer faces technical, social and economic problems at once and has to bind the solutions into a configuration that works. So they are simultaneously producing knowledge, social realities, and material things, and the constructions can be separated on paper and not in practice.

The strong version of the claim: builders of technology have to build the artefact and build the environment in which the artefact can function, and neither job can usually be done alone. They must combine raw materials, skills, knowledge and capital, which means enrolling a lot of actors who are not all immediately compatible.

And scientific work is the same. Actor-network theory treats scientists and engineers as separated only by disciplinary boundaries. Both build networks, the larger and more stable the better, out of laboratory equipment, established knowledge, patrons, money and institutions. No one piece of a network can determine the shape of the whole.

The word for the general case is co-production, and his examples are good ones. The criteria for good climate science are shaped by policy concerns while the criteria for good policy are shaped by climate science. Ideas about health and disease bend around standardised classifications of diseases and interventions. Assisted reproduction looks miraculous and works through mundane work integrating political, legal, ethical, bureaucratic, medical, technical and intensely personal domains at once.

Example · Box 6.3, how the Pap smear became the right tool for the job

It began somewhere else entirely. Papanicolaou published in 1917 on vaginal smears as a way of telling what stage of the oestrous cycle a guinea pig was in. Over the following decade he found he could also spot free-floating human cancer cells.

His 1928 presentation of that was met with little enthusiasm, for three separate reasons: the results were not convincing, pathologists were not used to looking at free-floating cells, and gynaecologists were not interested in cancer. He abandoned it for another decade.

What revived it was an institution. The American Cancer Society took up the test and created an environment that could support the long tinkering needed to fix it. And it was never fixed, exactly: the test still has chronic ambiguities about the nature of cancer, about which cells count as abnormal, and about how a slide should be read, and a false negative rate between 15 and 50 per cent depending on circumstances.

Here is the part that makes it heterogeneous construction and not simply a medical success story. The test became cheap enough to use as mass screening by gendering the division of labour. Technicians, mainly women, could be paid less than the mainly male pathologists, could be paid piecework, and could do some of the reading at home. That is what made the volume possible, and volume is what makes a screening test work at all.

And each fix produced the next problem. Volume meant eyestrain, and the combination of low status with life-and-death responsibility. Automated record keeping cut costs further. High rates of misreading created public pressure to rate and regulate the laboratories, and women's health activists got governments to limit how many slides one technician may read in a day, shutting down the worst of what were called Pap mills. Misreadings also pushed laboratories into working directly with clinics, developing local classifications in place of the standard scheme, and those local schemes test better.

Many material and social things had to be built for the test to succeed, and the social ones are not a footnote to the biology. They are why it exists.

6. The construction of kinds

An old philosophical fight, imported. Are natural kinds part of the world, or only part of our classifying? The traditional version is about universals, the abstractions that range over individual things, like redness.

  • Nominalists hold that kinds are human impositions, even where classifying is easy and everyone agrees. Only individual objects are real. Their difficulty with the other view: where would a general property exist, and how would it attach to an object?
  • Realists hold that kinds are real features of the world, even where their edges are fuzzy and their application partly conventional. Their difficulty with the other view: do we not discover features of the world, and if kinds were not real, why would classifying be worth anything?

Nominalism is one way of cashing out the construction metaphor. If kinds are not features of the world then they are made, and if they are made by groups of people then they are socially made.

And then the sentence that earns the chapter its place in this course. Since science is the most influential institution that classifies things, science is central to the social construction of reality. Deciding what counts as a species, a disease, a planet or a race is deciding what there is.

7. The construction of nature, which is the one everybody fights about

It is a short step from nominalism to the strongest claim in the field, and Sismondo takes the step and then refuses it. On this version, representations directly shape their objects: when scientists agree on a claim, they literally make it true. The world comes to match the agreement, with the arrow of dependence pointing that way. When engineers agree on the most efficient solution, that makes it the most efficient one.

He calls it neo-Kantian constructivism, because Kant held that human beings impose some structures on the world, and this is that with a social mind in place of an individual one.

Two things make it plausible, and they are not stupid.

  • There is no independent access. The natures of things are never available without a representation. When anyone agrees about anything they do so in response to sense experience, mediated information and arguments, and even sense experience is itself a response to something.
  • Contingency before stability. Close studies of science find a great deal of contingency in knowledge before it settles. Disagreement is the rule and not the exception. Yet objects behave themselves once agreement is reached, and that change needs explaining.
Science as something already in existence, already completed, is the most objective, impersonal thing that we humans know. Science as something coming into being, as a goal, is just as subjectively psychologically conditioned as are all other human endeavors.
Einstein, quoted on page 68. Finished science and science in progress look like different activities, and this whole field is the study of the second one.

Steve Woolgar states the position most directly, arguing that the existence and character of a discovered object differ according to the social network, that the network does not merely mediate between the object and the observation but constitutes the object, and that the relationship therefore inverts: the representation gives rise to the object.

Sismondo's objection is a question about mechanism, and it is devastating precisely because it is simple. Kant's version was modest because it was individual: a person imposes structure as they apprehend the world, and there is nothing to explain. STS cannot be modest in that way, because its whole emphasis is social. So: how does consensus affect material reality? How do the convictions of authorities carry weight with the world that the convictions of non-authorities do not? How does agreement cause a change in matter?

If it were true, representation would act at a distance with no mediators. A successful representation would change or create the thing it represents, with no causal connection running from one to the other. That violates fundamental assumptions about cause and effect, and it is why Latour says social constructivism is not plausible for more than a second.

Then a political objection, and it is the one worth carrying into a seminar. Feminists point out that science's images of women are sometimes sexist, especially in how fast they naturalise gender. If neo-Kantian constructivism were right, a feminist critic could try to change science's construction of women but could not call it false, because scientific consensus would be true by definition. The same trap catches environmentalists: they could argue about the dominant view of how resilient nature is, and never say it was wrong. The strongest form of the constructivist claim disarms exactly the people who most want to use it.

And then he half gives it back. Given that several of the other six senses are obviously true, neo-Kantian constructivism may be a decent approximation and methodologically useful even if it is wrong as metaphysics. Talk about the social construction of reality may amount to little more than metaphor or sweeping language, and as a metaphor it does a job: it draws attention to contingency, and sends researchers off to ask what caused it.

Context: Box 6.4, and why this matters outside the seminar room

The worry. Some people in environmental studies argue that constructivism undercuts scientific knowledge. Public trust in science rests on a picture of formulaic methods uncovering truths about nature, and STS keeps showing that other circumstances would have produced other knowledge. So constructivism looks like it cripples science's ability to underwrite environmental politics.

Sismondo says that is a misdiagnosis, and his reason is empirical. Environmental politics already pits experts against each other. Each side presents its own view as entirely forced by nature and reason, with no room for disagreement, while finding all the places the other side's argument was open to choices. So the fact that scientific knowledge is laden with choices is not a secret that STS is letting out. It is rediscovered in every dispute.

The constructive conclusion. Science should be built with controversy already in mind, and its authority should not rest on a formal picture of itself that is incorrect, because resting on that means being rejected the moment the picture fails. Science solves more public problems when its knowledge is adjusted to its public context and attuned to what other people know.

The conclusion, which is more conciliatory than the reputation of the phrase

The phrase escaped the field. Construction talk is now used in psychology, geography, environmental studies, education, management, cultural studies and even accounting, and STS is partly responsible: because scientific knowledge is normally taken to reflect the natural world with scientists as passive conduits, saying that scientific realities are constructed was a genuinely radical claim, and radical claims travel.

But the diversity means there is no neat theory there. What survives is the three reminders: science and technology are social, they are active, and they do not take nature as it comes.

And the closing position, which is the thing to quote if anyone accuses this field of relativism. Some of these forms are controversial in principle and all are controversial in application, but given their diversity even the staunchest realist cannot dismiss constructivist claims out of hand. Constructivism is the study of how scientists and technologists build socially situated knowledge and things, and such studies can show how scientists build good representations of the material world, in a perfectly ordinary sense.

He then says where science's power comes from, in his own voice: its ability to manipulate nature and measure nature's reactions, and its ability to carry those measurements across time and space to other laboratories and other contexts. Laboratory technologies contribute to objectivity and to objective knowledge.

So constructivist STS may even support a version of realism. Not the idea that anybody has unmediated knowledge of reality, and not the idea that there is one complete set of truths. But a version.

The Pap smear was rejected because gynaecologists were not interested in cancer

That line in Box 6.3 is easy to skim past and it is remarkable. In 1928 a test that could detect cervical cancer early failed to interest the specialty that treats cervical cancer, because early detection was not what that specialty thought it was for. Cancer was something you found when it presented, and by then the useful question was surgical.

The estimate usually given is that the test has cut deaths from cervical cancer in developed countries by around seventy per cent since it came into routine use. It sat unused for ten years for reasons that had nothing to do with whether it worked.

There is a real argument about what Mendel's numbers show

In 1936 the statistician and geneticist R. A. Fisher reanalysed Mendel's published data and found it fitted the expected ratios too well: the deviations were smaller than chance should have produced, by a margin he put at about one in thirty thousand.

Fisher did not accuse Mendel of faking it. His suggestion was that an assistant, knowing what was expected, had helped the counts along. The argument has run ever since, with defences ranging from sampling procedures that would bias the counts innocently, to the observation that classifying a pea as wrinkled or round involves judgement calls, and judgement drifts toward what you expect. Which is Box 2.3 from last week, the theory-dependence of observation, showing up in a monastery garden.

The Science Wars, and a hoax that is still being argued about

The fierce arguments Sismondo mentions in his first paragraph have a name. In 1996 the physicist Alan Sokal submitted an article to the cultural studies journal Social Text arguing that quantum gravity was a social and linguistic construct. It was deliberate nonsense, stuffed with fashionable citations, and they published it. He announced the hoax the same day in another magazine.

What it proved is still disputed. Sokal's supporters said it showed a field with no standards. Critics pointed out that Social Text was not peer reviewed at the time and that one editor waving through a physicist writing about physics proves little about the arguments themselves. Almost nobody in the field held the position Sokal parodied, which is Sismondo's sense 7, and Latour, the most famous name attached to it, had already rejected it.

Latour spent his last years worrying he had helped the wrong people

Bruno Latour, whose line about implausibility Sismondo quotes, wrote an essay in 2004 called "Why Has Critique Run out of Steam?" It opens with him reading about climate change denial and recognising the argumentative moves: that the science is not settled, that there is a lack of scientific certainty, that other interests are at work.

His worry was that a generation had been trained to expose facts as socially constructed, and that the technique had been picked up by people using it to defend industries against inconvenient findings. His answer was not to recant but to change the task: critique should be about assembling and protecting the things that matter, with debunking set aside. He died in 2022.

Berger and Luckmann were writing about something much less exciting

The Social Construction of Reality has almost nothing to do with science. It is a 1966 book in the sociology of knowledge, and it is mostly about how ordinary social arrangements come to feel like facts of nature: queueing, marriage, money, what counts as a rude question.

Their machinery is three-part. Externalisation, people act and make institutions. Objectivation, the institutions come to seem like external things with their own reality. Internalisation, the next generation is brought up inside them and experiences them as simply how the world is. STS borrowed the title and applied the machinery to laboratories, which the authors had not had in mind.

Glossary

Every term defined in this guide, collected and grouped by the chapter it comes from. Definitions are repeated here on purpose, so nothing has to be hunted for.

Stratificationch. 4

stratification
The fact that science is layered: a small minority of people produce most of the papers and receive most of the citations. Roughly 10 per cent of authors produce half of all papers.
the Matthew Effect
Merton's name, from Matthew 13:12, for the way scientific advantage compounds. A small early edge produces a better mentor, a better department, more visibility, more citations, and citation is itself a reward that produces more work.
cumulative advantage
The general form of the same idea, and the reason Sismondo likes it: differences too small to call unfair at any one step end up producing differences large enough to be undeniable.
rational reconstruction
What a scientific paper is, as against a record of what happened. Details cleaned up, idiosyncrasies removed, order of events rearranged to make the argument work. Why citation counts measure persuasion, and not influence.
the pipeline
The standard metaphor for women leaving science, with leaks at every joint. Sismondo accepts it only if intake, blockage and filters are attended to as well as leakage, since in many fields there are few women at the source to begin with.
getting in, staying in, getting on
Sara Delamont's three problems, and the three stages the chapter uses in place of the pipeline.
the unofficial PhD programme
The second curriculum that runs beside the official one: study groups, informal mentoring, the conversation in the corridor. Being socially included in it does not guarantee being technically included in it.
feminisation and status
Rossiter's finding that a field's status falls as women enter it and rises as men do, which is why women could enter science most easily through new fields that were then poorly regarded.

Constructionch. 6

social construction, the three reminders
Whatever else it claims, it reminds us that science and technology are social, that they are active, and that they do not run straight from nature to ideas about nature.
realism
The intuition that truths depend more on the natural world than on the people stating them. Sismondo notes it is usually a negative position, defined against whichever anti-realism is in front of it.
the Berger and Luckmann conditions
What it takes to construct a social X: knowledge of X shapes behaviour toward X, knowledge of X is common, and knowledge of X is transmitted. Then X cannot be wished away, and so it exists.
attributional model of discovery
Brannigan's claim that discoveries are not events in themselves but events retrospectively recognised as origins. Mendel discovered his laws in 1866 as a result of what happened in 1900.
creating phenomena
The laboratory sense of construction: materials arrive purified and standardised, objects are put in artificial situations, and new stable objects of study are manufactured. Nature is excluded from the laboratory, never brought into it.
heterogeneous engineering
Law's term. Building an artefact and building the environment in which it can work are one job, because neither can be done alone.
co-production
Science and social order shaping each other at the same time. The criteria for good climate science are shaped by policy while the criteria for good policy are shaped by climate science.
nominalism
The view that kinds are human impositions and only individual objects are real. Its opposite here is realism about kinds. Nominalism is one way of cashing out the construction metaphor.
neo-Kantian constructivism
The strongest sense: when scientists agree on a claim they literally make it true. Sismondo rejects it, because it requires representation to act at a distance with no causal connection.
