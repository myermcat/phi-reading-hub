Who Decided That Thin Was Better?

In 1894 American newspapers ran a panic about an epidemic of thinness, and doctors wrote books warning that slender women were a threat to the nation. A century later the same country is running a panic about an epidemic of fatness, and poor black women are the ones blamed for it. Strings argues that the verdict flipped because it was never about health. It was built out of two things: race science made during the slave trade, which tied fatness to Africans, and Protestant preaching, which made eating a sin. Doctors arrived afterwards and ratified what was already there.

Strings, the introduction and chapter 3

Introduction, printed pages 1 to 10, and chapter 3, printed pages 54 to 83. The introduction is the whole book's argument in ten pages and is the part an exam question would use. Chapter 3 is a chain of five men between 1684 and 1815, each one adding a piece, and it reads as history.

The medical verdict on body weight reversed completely in about a century, so the body was never what was setting it

The claim that does all the work Fat phobia was in place before medicine took a position on fat. It was assembled out of race science and Protestant preaching between about 1680 and 1900, and doctors joined in afterwards. So a health explanation of why thinness is admired has the order of events backwards.

Start with the newspaper, because the newspaper is the surprising part. On 16 February 1894 the New York Times ran the headline “Actually Starving! A Prominent New York Man Dies in Sight of Food.” The article claimed that thousands of New Yorkers were starving while having plenty of money for food, and quoted a physician saying they were dying slowly and looked like consumptives, meaning like people with tuberculosis. Doctors of the period wrote about the pale, thin and puny bodies they saw everywhere, about narrow chests and flabby muscles. Thinness was the epidemic.

The worry was loudest about women, and it was patriotic. Dr William Alcott, in The Young Woman's Book of Health of 1855, warned that a whole generation of American women was being trained to feebleness and want of appetite. Dr John Harvey Kellogg, the Michigan sanitarium doctor who later sold breakfast cereal, wrote that American girls were lacking in physical development and needed a nutritional revolution because their eating produced scrawny bodies. In 1888 the Washington Post asked whether girls were growing smaller and complained that English and German women weighed more, and that American stock could not hold its own against the big-boned foreigner.

YearThe epidemicWho is blamed
1894ThinnessWell-off white women, for starving themselves and weakening the national stock
2019FatnessPoor black women, for burdening the public health system

Now the fact that makes it a philosophy problem. Wealthy American women were trying to be thin during the exact years doctors were attacking thinness as dangerous. The historian Adele Clarke's phrase for it is that fashionable women were wasting in style. So the thin ideal was running against the medical advice of its own moment, which means something other than health was driving it.

The 1896 magazine article that gives the answer away. Harper's Bazaar ran a piece called “Are Our Women Scrawny?”, defending American women against the charge of being sallow and thin. Praising the new roundness of the modern girl, the anonymous author still let slip where the limit was, calling the opposite extreme menacing, and then named the comparison outright: stoutness and surplus flesh are never desirable except among African savages.

Stoutness, corpulence, and surplusage of flesh are never desirable except among African savages.
“Are Our Women Scrawny?”, Harper’s Bazaar, November 1896, quoted by Strings, printed p. 3

That sentence is the book in miniature. A magazine giving beauty advice in 1896 reaches for Africa to explain why a reader should not get fat, and expects the reader to understand. Strings's question is where that reflex came from.

Her thesis in one sentence. Two developments built the fear of fat: the transatlantic slave trade, whose race science tied fatness to greedy Africans, and the spread of Protestantism, whose preachers made overeating ungodly. The two circulated separately in western Europe for two centuries and fused in the United States in the early nineteenth century, in a country holding slaves, running religious revivals, and taking in immigrants its elites classed as part African.

And the medical part came last. Only after those associations were settled did the medical establishment take up excess fat as a public health campaign. Which is why Strings says the preference for thinness has not principally been about health, and has been one of the ways a body gets used to hold race, sex and class rankings in place.

INTROThe Original Epidemic

Sabrina Strings, Fearing the Black Body: The Racial Origins of Fat Phobia, New York University Press, 2019
Read properly10 pp, printed pages 1 to 10
What you get out of this chapter. A documented case of a medical consensus reversing completely inside a hundred years, with the newspaper headlines on both ends of it. A two-cause thesis stated in one sentence, which is the most quotable thing on the reading list. The two theorists she borrows and what each one does for her, Bourdieu on how elites invent tastes to separate themselves from the poor and Foucault on how medicine tells people how to live. The 1894 panic was about thinness, and the doctors were leading it

The question If fat is a health problem, why did American doctors spend the nineteenth century warning that women were dangerously thin?

The answer is that the medical position on body weight has reversed inside about a hundred years, which is too fast for it to be a discovery about bodies. Strings opens with the 16 February 1894 New York Times: “Actually Starving! A Prominent New York Man Dies in Sight of Food.” The article claims thousands of New Yorkers are starving to death while able to buy the best food, and quotes a physician saying the sufferers are middle and upper class and look emaciated.

The doctors of the period wrote the same thing in books. They described pale, thin and puny bodies proliferating, and narrow chests and lank limbs and flabby muscles at every corner. Thinness was described in the vocabulary now reserved for fat: an epidemic, a national failing, a thing a doctor must intervene in.

The women got the strongest version of it. William Alcott, a New England physician and a distant relative of the novelist Louisa May Alcott, published The Young Woman's Book of Health in 1855 and warned about a generation of women trained to tenderness, delicacy, nervousness, feebleness of muscle and want of appetite. Being tall, slender and delicate, he said, does not prepare a young woman for what life will do to her. John Harvey Kellogg wrote in his Ladies' Guide in Health and Disease that American girls were decidedly lacking in physical development and needed a nutritional revolution, because their eating produced bodies he called scrawny and waspish.

Example · thinness as a threat to the nation

The Washington Post, 1888, under the headline “Are Girls Growing Smaller?”: the girl of the period ranges from 140 pounds down to 80 pounds or less, and in England and Germany the figures are higher. Eighty pounds of femininity, the writer adds, is not much.

Then the step that makes it political. American women will go on getting thinner until they disappear, it has happened in Boston already, and American stock cannot hold its own against the big-boned strong-built foreigner: the Irish have crowded the Yankee out of New England.

What to notice. Weight is already standing in for racial and national ranking in 1888, with the verdict pointing the opposite way from today. Thin means the native-born stock is losing. The same reasoning with the sign flipped is what a reader meets in 2026.

Rich women were slimming while doctors told them to stop, so health was not what was driving it

The question Were nineteenth-century women thin because nobody had told them how to eat?

No. They were thin on purpose, against medical advice, which is the fact the whole book is built on. If the thin women of the 1890s had simply been uninformed, then better information would have made them heavier, and the story would be about nutrition. The evidence says otherwise: well-off women were working at being slender in the very years doctors were publishing against slenderness. The historian Adele Clarke's phrase is that women of the fashionable classes were wasting in style.

So the thin ideal was already strong enough to beat a doctor. Whatever it ran on, it was not the health advice of its own moment, because the health advice of its own moment said the opposite. Strings's word for what it ran on is a marker: for the women who adopted it, slenderness marked moral, racial and national superiority.

  • What the ignorance explanation predicts. Give a slender woman better information and she gains weight.
  • What happened. Doctors published that information loudly for fifty years and fashionable women went on slimming, so something was outbidding it.

And the 1896 magazine says so out loud. Harper's Bazaar, in “Are Our Women Scrawny?”, defends American women, grants that the modern girl has a wholesome glow and some flesh on her, and then warns that the famous American litheness is on the wane and the opposite extreme is menacing. Its closing rule is that stoutness, corpulence and surplus flesh are never desirable except among African savages.

Read what that sentence takes for granted. It assumes the reader already knows that fat belongs to Africans, that this is a bad thing to be, and that naming it will settle the question of whether to gain weight. Nobody explains any of it, which means by 1896 it had stopped needing explanation. Chapter 3 is the history of how it got that way.

Her thesis: the slave trade and Protestantism built it, and doctors arrived last

The question Where did the fear of fat come from, if it did not come from medicine?

From two places, and Strings names them in one sentence. Two historical developments produced a fetish for slimness and a phobia about fatness: the rise of the transatlantic slave trade, whose race science tied fatness to greedy Africans, and the spread of Protestantism, whose preachers held that overeating was ungodly.

The two ran separately for two centuries, and then fused in one country. Both ideas were loose in western Europe from the seventeenth century. They came together as a single coherent position in the United States in the early nineteenth century, in the particular conditions there: a slave economy, a wave of religious revivals, and mass immigration of people whom American elites classed as part African, meaning the Irish first and southern and eastern Europeans later. In that setting fatness became stigmatised as black and as sinful at the same time, and by the early twentieth century popular magazines were promoting slenderness as the correct body for a white Anglo-Saxon Protestant woman.

Only then did the doctors mobilise. The medical establishment began its campaign against excess fat after those associations were in place. Which gives Strings her conclusion: the phobia about fatness and the preference for thinness have not principally or historically been about health, and have been one of the ways a body is used to make race, sex and class hierarchies look natural.

Race degrades black women and disciplines white women in the same move

The question Which group was being disciplined by all this?

Both groups at once, and Strings says most writing on race and beauty misses this because it picks one group and describes their experience. Her phrase for it is that race works as a double agent. The same discourse that called fatness coarse, immoral and black degraded black women, and it was also what made slenderness the required body for elite white Christian women. One idea does the repressing and the disciplining together, which is why the thin ideal and fat phobia are one development and not two.

Say what that means concretely. A white woman in 1890 refusing a second helping is doing something about black women even if there is none in the room, because the reason the second helping is shameful is a racial ranking she has absorbed. The discipline is applied to her, and the degradation is applied to somebody else, by the same sentence.

Two theorists do the lifting, and she says which part each one carries.

  1. Pierre Bourdieu, for how a taste gets invented. Bourdieu was a French sociologist whose claim is that elites work constantly to separate themselves from the people below them, and do it by cultivating tastes, diets and appearances opposed to those of the groups they want distance from. He called these social distinctions, and their work is to make a social ranking look like a natural difference in refinement. Strings uses him for the race part: elite Europeans and white Americans used racial talk to mark themselves off from supposedly greedy and fat others, meaning black people and also the whites they classed as degraded or hybrid, the Celtic Irish, southern Italians and Russians.
  2. Michel Foucault, for how medicine finishes the job. Week 8 had him. Medicine becomes the institution of the twentieth century that tells people how to live for health and long life, and the practices a person performs to count as healthy are what Foucault called the biopolitics of health management. Strings adds the part that matters here: that regime is not applied evenly. It is held out as an obligation to dominant groups and withheld from poor and racially othered ones, which keeps the existing ranking in place while looking like public health.

And it was women on both counts. Strings leans on gender scholarship for the step that since women have long been assessed largely on how they look, a racial and moral ranking attached to bodies falls hardest on the women of each group.

What is new in her book, since an exam may ask. Amy Farrell's Fat Shame of 2011 connected fat stigma and the thin ideal to race. R. Marie Griffith's Born Again Bodies of 2004 traced the Christian preaching against gluttony. What none of them did, on Strings's account, was a history: the named people who spread these ideas, in order, with the political conditions that made each step take.

If you keep one thing. The medical verdict on body weight reversed inside a century, from a panic about thin American women in the 1890s to a panic about fat black women now. A verdict that can flip that fast was not being set by the bodies, and Strings's answer for what was setting it is the slave trade and Protestantism, with medicine arriving last to confirm a ranking that was already in place.
My noteThe introduction is the examinable half. The two-cause sentence, the slave trade and Protestantism, is the single most useful line in the week and takes ten words to write out. Have the 1894 starvation headline and the 1896 Harper's Bazaar line about African savages ready as the pair that shows the reversal, since one answer needs both ends of it. Know which theorist does which job: Bourdieu for how elites invent a taste to mark themselves off, Foucault for how medicine then issues it as health advice to some people and withholds it from others.
Where it belongs in the quarrel
  • It answers week 7. That week argued science cannot be value free and asked who chooses the values. Here the choosing is visible: the values were in place before the science, and the science arrived to agree with them.
  • It extends week 8. Tremain argued that normal is a nineteenth-century medical word that hides a judgment inside a description. A weight chart is that word applied to bodies, and Strings dates where its content came from.

Kellogg, who wanted American women to gain weight, invented corn flakes

John Harvey Kellogg ran the Battle Creek Sanitarium in Michigan, a health resort where the wealthy went for diet regimes, hydrotherapy and exercise. The breakfast cereal came out of the kitchen there, and his brother Will turned it into the company.

Strings profiles him in chapter 7, which is not assigned. His position in the 1890s was that elite American women were too thin and that this threatened the nation, which is the opposite of what the company carrying his name now sells cereal on.

Bernier invents race in 1684, and spends half the document rating women

The question Who first sorted humanity into races by skin colour, and what else was in the document?

François Bernier, a French physician, in a three-page letter of 1684, and nearly half of it is about which women are good-looking. That second fact is the one Strings is after. Earlier scholars treated the beauty section as padding. Her argument is that judging women's looks was part of race-making from the first document there ever was, because a beautiful woman was meant to be evidence that her race was superior.

Who he was. Born in 1625 to farmers in Anjou, sent to Paris at fifteen, where he studied under Pierre Gassendi, a priest and philosopher known for opposing Descartes. He took a medical degree at Montpellier in three months, on condition that he never practise in France. In 1658 he went to India and stayed twelve years as private physician to Prince Dara Shikoh and then to Dara's brother and rival Aurangzeb, and wrote it up as Travels in the Mogul Empire.

Context What the word race had meant before 1684

The word was medieval and had been in use for centuries. It marked a lineage or a stock inside a society: a noble house, a breed of horse, the Jewish or Moorish subjects of a crown. It attached to some people and left others unmarked.

What he did that was new. Travel writers before him described the people they met. Bernier sorted them, by skin colour, and used the word race for the groups he made. After him every person on earth has one, readable off where they live and what they look like.

The scheme itself, which is incoherent and was still used for centuries. He could not settle how many races there were and wrote “four or five.”

  1. The first. Europe, north Africa from Morocco to Tripoli, much of Asia, and, after some hesitation, the indigenous people of the Americas.
  2. The second. The rest of Africa.
  3. The third. China, Japan, much of east Asia, Uzbekistan and the Turkomans.
  4. The fourth, alone. The Lapps of Scandinavia.

The clause that mattered most was eight words long. Bernier wrote that the black skin of Africans came from their sperm and blood. He said it almost in passing, and it is the sentence that puts the difference inside the body. Whatever else he was unsure about, he held that white people were physiologically distinct from black people and that this inner difference produced the outward one.

He never argued for slavery, and that is why the step is worth watching. Bernier did not come out in favour of African slavery anywhere in the letter. But he was writing in Paris in the 1680s, in a salon full of the king's courtiers where one of the standing debates was whether some groups of people were a different species and therefore natural slaves. In that room, a scheme that puts white skin in a first race and black skin in a second carries a ladder with it, whether or not the man who drew it meant one. Later writers who did want the slave trade extended picked it up and used it.

Example · the law that made the classification pay

France had kept a Freedom Principle since 1315: nobody could be held as a forced labourer on French soil. It said nothing about the colonies, so the king was freeing slaves at home while licensing slavery abroad. Africans who reached France petitioned for freedom and generally got it.

The Edict of October 1716 let slave holders bring slaves to France if they registered them at a courthouse. The Declaration of 1738 went further: an unregistered slave would be seized and shipped back to the colonies. Both laws were written to apply specifically to nègres, African slaves.

What that did to a courtroom. A person suing for freedom could win by proving they were not African. Proof meant physical traits, and the list of physical traits was the one Bernier and his successors had drawn up. A classification scheme written in a salon became the thing that decided whether a named person went back into slavery.

Buffon adds body size in 1749, and invents the plump, stupid African

The question When did body size become a racial marker, and what had it been before?

In 1749, with Buffon, and before him the European stereotype of Africans had been that they were small and thin. Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon, published Histoire naturelle in that year, with a chapter called “Of the Varieties in the Human Species.” He agreed with Bernier that colour was the first difference between races, and named the second: the size and shape of the body.

What he was correcting, and what he put in its place. He knew the English stereotype, which he credited to the seafarer Francis Drake, that Africans were black, meagre and very small. He called it inaccurate, and sorted it: the short, meagre people were Moors, and Moors belonged to the race of whites. Black Africans, he wrote, were tall, plump, and simple and stupid.

So a stereotype was not replaced, it was split. Buffon kept the small-and-thin picture that was already in European heads and assigned it to a different group, which left him free to give black Africans a new physical identity made of dark skin and bulk. Barbara and Karen Fields have a word for this kind of manufacture, racecraft, and Strings uses it here.

His explanation for the plumpness was laziness, and it had a climate theory attached. The land of the Negroes, he wrote, was rich, full of pasture and millet and evergreen trees, so black Africans could stay well fed with no effort, which made them well fed and also simple and stupid. The chain runs: easy food, no work, no thought, fat body.

He did not invent the link between fat and dullness. He imported it. Buffon was elected to the Royal Society on a visit to England, and the Society's journal had been carrying that idea for a century. One typical entry from 1733, by a Dr Alexander Stuart, answers a question about why people feel sleepy after eating. Stuart treats sleepiness as laziness, blames overeating and a lack of self-management, and says it is gross feeders, drunkards and the corpulent who feel it, while temperate persons never do. He adds that the problem is endemic in hot climates, which make their inhabitants lazy and inactive.

Put the two together and Buffon's sentence assembles itself. English natural philosophy already held that fat means a dull mind and that heat makes people idle. Buffon applied both to a continent and produced a race that is fat because it is idle and idle because it is hot.

Both of the first two race theorists called black women beautiful

The question Did race science start out by calling black women ugly?

No, and this is the finding that makes the chapter worth reading, because it means the degradation was added later and can be dated. Bernier, in the same letter that sorted humanity by colour, wrote that among the blacks of Africa he had seen women of such astonishing beauty that they put the Venus of the Palazzo Farnese in Rome in the shade. He listed the features: aquiline nose, small mouth, coral lips, ivory teeth, large bright eyes, gentle features.

I have encountered seven or eight in various places who were of such an astonishing beauty that they put in the shade the Venus of the Palazzo Farnese in Rome.
François Bernier, “A New Division of the Earth”, 1684, quoted by Strings, printed p. 59

Read the list again, because the praise has a catch in it. Every item is the existing European standard. The black women he calls beautiful qualify by resembling a Roman statue, and the ones he excludes are the ones with thick lips and a snub nose. So the compliment is paid in somebody else's currency, and the standard is never in question.

And the setting of that sentence should be said plainly. Bernier wrote that at Mokha he saw several of these women completely naked, waiting to be sold, and that nothing in the world could be lovelier to see. He is admiring women at a slave market. The aesthetic judgment and the trade are in the same sentence.

Buffon does the same thing sixty-five years later. He called the physique of black Africans well made, and wrote several pages on the beauty of black women of particular nations. Of the Senegalese, whom he thought the most attractive, he wrote that they were tall, very black, well proportioned, with features less harsh than other Africans, and that apart from colour they were as handsome as women anywhere in the world.

So what were these men doing, if the project was supposed to rank people? Strings's answer is that the early schemes were trying to make sense of the variety the travellers were reporting, and that they were hierarchical without being total. A woman could be racially inferior on the scheme and still count as desirable. Condemning black people in every category came later, and it came with a reason: as the French and British slave trades grew, claims about inborn differences in intelligence, industry and appetite became useful as evidence that the people being enslaved deserved it.

Which is why the beauty passages matter for an exam answer. They are the dated proof that the content of a racial category changes to fit what the people using it need. Nothing in a body changed between 1684 and 1837. What changed was the size of the trade that the category was being asked to justify.

[Those] given up to the pleasures of the table, those great eaters, intemperate epicures who seem to live only to eat, have a stupid look … always digesting, they become incapable of thinking.
Julien-Joseph Virey on black Africans, Natural History of the Negro Race, 1837, quoted by Strings, printed p. 74

In plain words: eating has filled the place where thinking would have gone. Three pieces of it:

  1. Given up to the pleasures of the table. A surrender, which means somebody could have held out and this person did not. The moral charge Le Romain added is now being treated as observation.
  2. Always digesting. A physical process is doing the explaining, so the claim is about the body and no account of a person's life can touch it.
  3. Incapable of thinking. The conclusion is permanent and it is about the whole group. Nothing a member of the group does can count against it.

He put the same claim into a skull. Virey wrote, in words worth paraphrasing closely, that the white forehead juts forward and the white mouth draws back, as though whites were designed for thought, while in the negro the forehead retreats and the mouth projects, as though he were made for eating. A shape is being read as a purpose, which is the same move the Phineas Gage story made in week 8.

What it rules out. On this picture a black person who thinks is an exception to their own anatomy. Evidence against the claim has been disqualified in advance, which is what makes it a scientific-looking statement that nothing could refute.

The Encyclopédie turns idle eating into gluttony, which is a sin

The question How did a claim about laziness become a claim about immorality?

By one word changing hands between two friends, and the word is gluttony. Buffon's account of black bodies had no moral charge in it: the food was easy to get and the people lacked the mental capacity to do anything other than eat. That is a claim about circumstances and intelligence. It says nothing about guilt.

Denis Diderot was Buffon's friend and was building the Encyclopédie. Diderot had published Letters on the Blind in 1749 and been imprisoned for it, and on release he lowered the volume of his attacks on the church and set about a compendium of everything rational inquiry had produced, which would undermine the church's authority without naming it. He took the entries on race from Buffon's Natural History.

For the longer entry on blacks he used a friend, Jean-Baptiste-Pierre Le Romain, who had lived in the Caribbean and presented himself as an expert on French colonies and sugar plantations. Le Romain's text calls the Senegalese the most attractive in Africa, which he lifted straight from Buffon, and then turns: their penchant for pleasure makes them unfit for hard labour, they are lazy, cowardly and very fond of gluttony, and the least esteemed are the Bambaras, who are lazy, drunken, gluttonous and apt to steal.

  • Buffon's version. Food is abundant, the people lack the capacity for anything else, so they eat and grow large. The cause is outside the person.
  • Le Romain's version. They are fond of gluttony. The cause is now a desire the person has and could have resisted.

Gluttony is not a Buffon word, and it is not a neutral one. It is one of the seven deadly sins. Where Buffon had described people eating because food was there, Le Romain describes people choosing to eat too much, which makes the fatness a fault and its owner blameworthy. Strings notes the irony, since Diderot was an atheist and the book was meant to displace the church, and a category straight out of Christian moral teaching went into it anyway.

Reports of African overfeeding had existed for a century, and they had been travellers' tales. In the 1580s the Italian botanist Prospero Alpini described an art of fattening in Egypt, where he claimed women took drugs and ate fat-laden food to become as round as possible, and called it a shameful vice of the flesh. In 1625 the Englishman Samuel Purchas published accounts of the inhabitants of Guinea as greedy eaters, no less drinkers, lecherous, thievish and without knowledge of God.

What the Encyclopédie changed was the status of such reports. Before it, they were sensational stories about strange customs. After it, they were entries in the reference work that educated Europe used, read by intellectuals and by men with commercial interests in Africa until the Revolution. Strings's phrase is that these narratives were becoming a type of gospel.

And the British were writing the same thing. Edward Long's History of Jamaica of 1774 said that people from Negro-land have no moral sensations and no taste except for women, gourmandising and drinking to excess, and wish to be idle. Long idolised Buffon and cited him throughout, and like Buffon he described many blacks as large, fat and well-proportioned, with the women worth praising. His one departure was that he was a polygenist, meaning he held that the human races have separate origins, and he used the idle appetite and the size as evidence for it.

Virey puts it inside the body, with bile, and now it cannot be argued with

The question What happens to the argument when the cause moves from the climate into the flesh?

It stops being answerable, because a person cannot leave their own bile. Buffon's explanation had been environmental: hot country, easy food, idle people. On that account moving somebody would change them. Julien-Joseph Virey, a French naturalist and army pharmacist who republished Buffon's Natural History in 1799 and wrote his own Histoire naturelle du genre humain in 1801, kept Buffon's conclusions and changed the cause underneath them.

The theory he used was humoral and it was already old. Humoral medicine, which goes back to the ancient Greeks, held that the body runs on four fluids and that illness is an imbalance of them. One of the four is black bile. The revived version said that black skin is caused by a surplus of black bile under it, and that the same surplus causes digestive disorders and weight gain. One fluid now explains colour and fatness together, which is exactly what a race theorist needed.

What he wrote with it. Black people were given up to the pleasures of the table, great eaters, intemperate epicures who seem to live only to eat, always digesting and therefore incapable of thinking. He claimed a direct correlation: those who are darker than others of the same race are also more robust, active and stout, because the hot sun makes the body hold onto excess liquid fat, which gathers in the breast and belly.

His example was the Khoikhoi, called Hottentot, and the choice is strange because it contradicts him. The word Hottentot was a derogatory name invented by Dutch settlers for the Khoikhoi of the Cape, and used loosely for the Khoisan generally. Every European account of them for two hundred years had called them thin.

  • Bernier, 1684. Smaller and thinner than other Africans.
  • Anders Sparrman, the Swedish physician who went to the Cape. As tall as most Europeans and generally more slender, because they were stinted and curtailed in their food.
  • John Hawkesworth, writing up Captain Cook's voyage, 1773. Of a slim make, and rather lean than plump.

Virey, who had never been there, described them as fat. He wrote that Khoikhoi women develop big bottoms and bellies as they age, that the climate makes the body retain liquid fat and the women are often sedentary or pregnant, so the fat collects in the abdomen, the breasts and around the hips, and that their rear ends resembled those of four-legged animals and could grow large enough to need a small cart to support them.

Two things turned a slender people into a fat one between 1773 and 1810. First, British writers began merging Hottentot with Negro, which handed the Khoikhoi the body already written for west Africans. Johann Caspar Lavater, the Swiss pastor who founded physiognomy, used the two words interchangeably and was widely read in Britain, and after the British took Cape Town in 1795 their reports described the Khoikhoi in the terms French and English authors had used for Negroes fifty years earlier. John Barrow's travel book of 1804 blamed the Dutch Boers' mistreatment for it and still produced the sentence: unwilling to work and unable to think, indulging every sensual appetite, the African peasant grows to an unwieldy size.

Second, and decisively, one woman was put on a stage in London.

Sara Baartman, and what was done to her

The question How did one person's body come to stand for a whole category?

Because she was exhibited as a specimen, and the billing said so in those words. Saartjie “Sara” Baartman was born around 1789, by most accounts, and raised on the farm of a colonist named David Fourie, descended from Huguenots who had fled Louis XIV and settled at the Cape in the 1680s. Fourie had taken the land, the cattle and the people on it by force. After his death the Baartman family was broken up and sold, and Sara and her parents went to a man named Cornelius Muller.

How she came to be on a stage. After a Khoikhoi rebellion in 1802 and the departure of many British, the Cape economy collapsed. A free black man named Hendrik, who had bought Sara after her parents died, was deeply in debt. He sold off a holding of some four hundred slaves, kept two, and decided to display Sara to British soldiers to pay down what he owed. From about 1806 she was shown at the Naval Hospital in Cape Town to soldiers and to dying men, who for a fee could touch her or have sex with her. The historians Clifton Crais and Pamela Scully call her an early nineteenth-century exotic dancer.

Context Her name, and what the sources did to it

Saartjie is a Dutch diminutive, the form a slaveholding household would use for a woman it owned. Sara is what she was called in Europe. Baartman was the family name, and the family had been broken up and sold after the colonist who held them died.

The handbills gave her none of these. They sold her as the Hottentot Venus, which is a slur joined to a Roman goddess, and that is the name under which she appears in the scientific literature for the next hundred and fifty years.

Then London. A Scottish surgeon called Alexander Dunlop saw commercial possibilities and got the illiterate Hendrik to sign a contract to accompany Sara to England as her showman. Sara was Hendrik's property and was not consulted. The three sailed in 1810. Dunlop's posters billed her as the most correct and perfect specimen of that race of people, and advertised that she possessed the kind of shape most admired among her countrymen, which is the explanation offered for calling her a Venus.

What the audiences came for. Londoners had seen displays of oversized people before: in 1803 Daniel Lambert, said to weigh over seven hundred pounds, exhibited himself as the fattest man alive. Sara's exhibition worked differently because she was presented as grotesque and exotic at once, a sexual curiosity with a racial label, and visitors paid extra to touch her. Reviewers described her figure as very different from the feminine standards of London and its ladies' long, slender lines.

The greatest curiousity in all the known world, the Wonderful and Surprising Hottentot Wenus is here, who measures three yards and three quarters round.
A ballad issued at the Bartholomew fair, London, 1812, quoted by Strings, printed p. 79

Some of them wanted a monster and complained when they did not get one. The Journal de Paris warned showgoers that ideas of beauty vary with climate and that they should not expect to find the Venus de Medici. One Frenchman wrote that he had gone hoping to see the imposing Venus of the Cape of Storms and had found only a svelte Venus. Strings's point is that the reports contradict each other, so the famous body was partly a thing the audience brought with them.

She lost everybody. Hendrik vanished a year into the stay in Europe. Dunlop died in 1812. In the summer of 1814 Sara went to France with a man named Henry Taylor, and nobody asked whether she was free, because French law by then banned the entry of all blacks, mulattoes and other people of colour under the Police des Noirs of 1777, and because Napoleon had reinstated slavery in 1802.

Context The French Revolution did not free anybody for five years, and then Napoleon undid it

France declared liberty and equality in 1789 and provisionally ended slavery in 1794, five years later. Strings gives the reason for the delay in one figure: by 1780 France's colonies were producing forty per cent of the Western world's sugar and coffee. The economy depended on it, so the question of black freedom was postponed.

Napoleon reinstated slavery in 1802. So Sara arrived in Paris in 1814 in a country that was legally a slave power and legally closed to black people, which is why her status was never in question there.

Cuvier dissects her in 1815, and fatness becomes a deformity

The question At what point does plumpness in a black woman stop being attractive and start being evidence of savagery?

With Georges Cuvier, in the autopsy report he wrote after Sara Baartman died in Paris in 1815. Cuvier ran the Museum of Natural History and was a national figure, the first person to establish that species go extinct. He was born in Württemberg to a strict Lutheran father and was brought into the Paris museum in 1795 by the zoologist Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire.

He turned down the first invitation and then saw her at a ball. Taylor had written to him inviting him to a show, and Cuvier refused. Some time later he saw Sara at a ball given by the Countess Du Barrier, where she was dressed in nothing but feathers, made to look like a bird. He then invited her to the museum, where she spent three days with artists on hand to sketch her. The one thing he most wanted to examine she refused him.

He got it after she died. Sara died in 1815, by Cuvier's account on 29 December. He obtained her body, recorded the cause of death as excessive drinking, and dissected her.

What he wrote is where the aesthetic verdict turns over. Buffon had called a large black female body well made. Cuvier, looking at one, wrote that her shape was all the more shocking because of the enormity of her hips, each measuring more than eighteen inches, and the projection of her buttocks, larger than half a foot. He called her hips and bottom her principal deformities. He noted that her knees were fat and a little knocked in, that her shoulders and back were graceful, that her arms were thin, and that her stomach did not protrude excessively.

Take the last one seriously, because it shows what the standard had become. A scientist recording that an abdomen failed to stick out too far is a scientist who expected it to and was relieved. Fat on a black woman's body is now a defect by default, and the measurements are being taken to confirm it.

He also decided she was not what she had been sold as. Cuvier concluded from the travel literature that Sara was a Bushman and no Khoikhoi at all, and then that she was an abnormal specimen even of that, since the accounts he had read described Bushmen as short, pockmarked and horribly thin. So he attributed her height and bulk to an abundance of food at the Cape, which is Buffon's explanation moved a hundred years and a continent.

Example · Cuvier was himself enormously fat

Elizabeth Kolbert, in The Sixth Extinction, records that Cuvier was thin during the French Revolution, grew stouter through the years he lived on the museum grounds, and became enormously fat towards the end of his life.

Strings marks it as speculation: he may have disliked in her what he disliked in himself, or he may have been reporting the rising feeling in France and England that fatness was uncharacteristic of white people.

Why it earns a place. It shows the standard already applying to the man applying it. The thin ideal is doing both of its jobs in one room: it condemns the woman on the dissecting table and it shames the scientist holding the knife.

What the chain of five men actually did

The question None of these men set out to make fat shameful, so how did they produce it?

Each one added a single piece, and no one of them held the finished idea. That is the chapter's shape, and it is the thing to be able to reproduce. Bernier made race a bodily fact. Buffon attached size to it. Le Romain in the Encyclopédie attached sin. Virey moved the cause inside the body. Cuvier made the result a deformity and did it over a corpse with a measuring tape.

Three features of the chain are worth having ready, because they are what an essay question can be answered with.

  1. Not one of them went and looked. Buffon never travelled to the parts of Africa he wrote about. Virey probably never met any of the women he described so confidently. Cuvier decided what a Bushman was from contradictory travel books. The one man in the chapter who did go to the Cape, Sparrman, is the one who reported that the Khoikhoi were slender and said it was because they were short of food.
  2. Each borrowed the last one's authority and changed the content. Le Romain copied Buffon's line about the Senegalese being the most attractive and then added gluttony, which Buffon had never written. Long quoted Buffon and Shakespeare in the same paragraph to argue for separate origins, which Buffon rejected. Virey republished Buffon and then replaced his environmental explanation with bile. The citation keeps the credibility while the claim underneath it moves.
  3. The claims track the slave trade. The hundred and thirty years of this chain are the peak years of the French and British trades. Strings's summary is that eating turned from a personal matter into a political one: an indulgence that philosophers had called a lowbrow habit of slow-witted people became evidence of low breeding, of an inborn race-specific appetite for ease, which is precisely what the men at the top of the new racial ranking were claiming to be free of.
If you keep one thing. The first two race theorists, Bernier in 1684 and Buffon in 1749, both called black women beautiful, and both praised plumpness in a woman. The contempt for fat black bodies was added afterwards, by men who had never left Europe, during the peak decades of the slave trade. So fat phobia has a start date and a reason, and a thing with a start date and a reason is not a fact about bodies.
My noteRevise this chapter as the five-name chain with one addition each, because the chapter is long and the chain is the whole of it: Bernier, Buffon, Le Romain, Virey, Cuvier, with 1684, 1749, 1750s, 1801, 1815. The two details that win marks on a question about whether racism is constant are the beauty passages in 3.3, since they are dated evidence that the category's content changed while nobody's body did. Sara Baartman is the case to use for any question about objectification or about scientific authority, and the name to spell right is Baartman. Virey's forehead and mouth quotation in 3.3 is the one line in the chapter worth memorising, because it shows a shape being read as a purpose. If a question asks how an idea spreads without anybody intending it, use the second point in 3.8: each writer cited the last one's authority and changed what was under it.
Where it belongs in the quarrel
  • It answers week 4. That week argued scientific facts get built through networks and citation. Here the network is visible and the citations are doing the building: Le Romain cites Buffon and adds a sin, Virey republishes Buffon and swaps out his cause.
  • It extends week 8. Tremain described an apparatus that produces the category it claims to find. Fat is one, and Strings supplies the dates and the names that Tremain's account describes in the abstract.
  • It is answered by anybody who treats racism as a constant. On that view the beauty passages are a puzzle or an exception. Strings treats them as the evidence, because they show the thing being built.
  • It is continued in chapters 4 to 8, which are not assigned: Protestant self-denial, the American adoption of the slender ideal, and the doctors who turned it into medicine.
For the curiousnone of this is on the exam
Bernier got his medical degree in three months and was banned from practising

He took the degree at the University of Montpellier in 1652 in about three months, on a condition attached to the fast-tracked qualification: he was not to practise medicine in the French commonwealth.

Which is part of why he went to India. He spent twelve years as a court physician in the Mughal empire, where the ban did not reach, and the travel writing he produced there is what made him an authority on foreign peoples when he came home.

The salon where race was invented was run by a woman arguing about embryology

Bernier wrote his three-page classification as a letter to Madame de la Sablière, whose Paris salon he belonged to after returning from India. She was an active participant in the debates of the day about the respective roles of men's semen and women's eggs in forming a child.

Strings suggests this is why Bernier's one line about black skin coming from sperm and blood was phrased as it was. He was writing for a reader who cared about that question.

Cuvier established that species go extinct, which was a real achievement

Comparing fossil elephant bones with living Indian and African elephants, Cuvier argued in 1796 that the fossils belonged to animals that no longer existed. Before him a missing species was assumed to be living somewhere unexplored.

Worth holding alongside the autopsy. The same man, the same method of careful comparative measurement, produced a lasting result in one case and a racist fabrication in the other, which is roughly the problem week 7 spent three chapters on.

Her remains were on display in Paris until 1974 and went home in 2002

Cuvier kept Sara Baartman's skeleton, her brain and her preserved genitals. They were held by the Musée de l'Homme in Paris and exhibited publicly into the 1970s.

Nelson Mandela asked France to return her in 1994. A French law authorising it passed in 2002, and she was buried at Hankey in the Eastern Cape on 9 August that year, South Africa's National Women's Day.

Glossary

fat phobia
Strings's subject: the fear of and aversion to fatness, which she argues is one development with the preference for thinness, and whose content is racial, religious, gendered and classed.
the thin ideal
The standard that a slender female body is the correct and admirable one. Strings dates its consolidation in the American mainstream to the early twentieth century and argues it was racialised from the start.
social distinctions
Pierre Bourdieu's term. Elites separate themselves from lower classes by cultivating tastes, diets and appearances opposed to theirs, which makes a social ranking look like a natural difference in refinement.
biopolitics
Foucault's term, used here for the way medicine tells people how to live for health and long life, and the practices a person must perform to count as a healthy citizen. Strings adds that the regime is applied to dominant groups and withheld from poor and racially othered ones.
race as a double agent
Strings's own phrase. One discourse does two jobs at once: it degrades black women by calling fatness coarse and immoral, and it disciplines white women by making slenderness the correct body for them.
process tracing
A comparative historical method. Follow a specific chain of named people and events through multiple sources to show how a result came about. Chapter 3 is one, running from 1684 to 1815.
race science
The field, taking off during the long eighteenth century, that classified humanity into physically distinct groups and ranked them. Strings's finding is that judging the beauty of each group's women was part of it from the first document.
racecraft
Barbara and Karen Fields's term for the work of manufacturing racial difference, as opposed to observing it. Buffon splitting the existing stereotype of small thin Africans off onto Moors, leaving black Africans with a new identity made of dark skin and bulk, is an instance.
polygenism
The position that the human races have separate origins and are effectively different species. Edward Long held it; Buffon rejected it; scholars disagree about Bernier.
humoral theory, black bile
Ancient Greek medicine, still in circulation in 1800, holding that the body runs on four fluids and that illness is an imbalance. The revived version Virey used claimed a surplus of black bile under the skin causes both dark colour and weight gain, which gave one inner cause for two things a race theorist wanted linked.
physiognomy
Reading character off facial and bodily form. Johann Caspar Lavater, a Swiss pastor, founded it in the 1770s and was widely read in Britain, and his interchangeable use of Hottentot and Negro helped merge the two categories.
Hottentot
A derogatory name invented by Dutch settlers for the Khoikhoi of the Cape, used loosely for the Khoisan generally. Used here because the sources use it, and worth knowing because the shift in how it was described, from slender to fat, is the chapter's turning point.
the Code Noir
The French royal decree of 1685 regulating slavery in the colonies, which condemned Africans there to lifelong servitude while France itself kept a principle that nobody could be held as a forced labourer on its soil.
Police des Noirs
Louis XVI's declaration of 1777, which banned the entry into France of all blacks, mulattoes and other people of colour, and so avoided the question of slavery on French soil by granting only white people the right to live there.
