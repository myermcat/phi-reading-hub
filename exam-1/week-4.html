  1. Stratification · do citations show influence

    1 Of the 55 Nobel laureates working in the United States in 1963, how many had studied or collaborated with a previous prize-winner, and what difference did it make to them?

    1. Both halves understate what the numbers show.

    2. Zuckerman 1977, quoted on her slide. Her conclusion from it: scientists tend to form elite groupings. The nine-year gap is the part to carry, because it is a measurable career advantage attached to who you trained with and not to what you found.

    3. The figure is 34, and the remaining 21 matter to the argument.

    4. Her reading of the numbers runs the other way.

    Her conclusion from the figure was that scientists form elite groupings. Which option supports that?

  2. Stratification · what Bacon expected

    2 Francis Bacon makes a second appearance in week 4. What did he expect from the inductive method, and how did it turn out?

    1. Bacon's method works by piling up particular cases and reasoning upward to a general law, which is called induction. This is why he is in this lecture. His promise was that a method anyone can follow makes genius unnecessary, so ordinary people working carefully would do the work. The distribution of output says something else happened.

    2. The opposite of his claim. His whole pitch was that method replaces genius.

    3. Not the claim in these notes.

    4. Not the expectation she quoted.

    Bacon's pitch was that a method anyone can follow makes a certain kind of person unnecessary. Which kind?

  3. Stratification · Cole and Cole on funding

  7. Stratification · cumulative advantage

    7 What is the cumulative advantage hypothesis, and what does it do to the picture of science as a meritocracy?

    1. A system where who gets ahead tracks how good they are is called a meritocracy, and this hypothesis is what puts that description of science in doubt. The loop in concrete terms: a good first post means a bigger lab, a bigger lab means more results, more results means a stronger grant, a stronger grant means a bigger lab again. Nobody in the loop is doing anything unfair, and the gap widens anyway. This is the week 4 idea most likely to carry a question.

    2. No such evening out is claimed.

    3. The reverse of the hypothesis.

    4. The hypothesis is offered as a challenge to exactly this reading.

    Four words, and the second two are the same as the first two.

  8. Discrimination · Rossiter

    8 Margaret Rossiter's historical study of women in American science found a link between who works in a field and how the field is regarded. What is it?

    1. The opposite direction.

    2. The 1982 finding underneath it is the sharp one: men perceive their own work as less masculine because women are present. So the status of the work tracks who is doing it, and not what the work is. Her examples of fields that feminised: sociology, economics, biology, chemistry.

    3. Not a claim in the lecture.

    4. This is the justification the finding undermines.

    The study is about what happens to a field's reputation when the people in it change.

    4. She described a direct effect.

  13. Discrimination · the maths gene

    13 Claims have been made about an inborn tendency, a biological propensity, explaining boys' better results in mathematics, and Harvard's president invoked biology in 2005. What was her verdict?

    1. The lecture rejects them.

    2. She gave a reason they fail, which is stronger than calling them untestable.

    3. A claim that states a political position while dressed as a finding is what she means by an ideological statement. Two separate objections here and it is worth holding both. The first is conceptual: there is no such thing as a genetic effect outside an environment, so a claim about genes alone is a claim about nothing. The second is empirical: the gap has been shrinking, and genes do not change in thirty years.

    4. Not a claim she made.

  15. Social construction · Hacking

    15 Ian Hacking wrote on constructivism in 1999. What is the core of constructivism set against realism, in her notes?

    1. The overstatement. Nothing in the lecture denies that theories answer to the world.

    2. The claim is about what success proves. A working theory shows that this arrangement works, and not that no other arrangement would have. That is why a constructivist can accept every result of science and still refuse the inference from it works to it is the truth.

    3. Instruments appear in the lecture and this is not the Hacking claim.

    4. The argument she presents applies to scientific facts too.

    The claim concerns what a theory's working proves, and what it leaves open.

  16. Social construction · realism

    16 What is the realist position, as her notes state it?

    1. This is the everyday use of the word realism and it is a different thing entirely. Worth separating, because the two meanings share one word.

    2. Three parts: the world has a definite way it is, we can find it out, and we can represent it accurately. A constructivist can accept the first and still dispute the second and third.

    3. Closer to positivism, the doctrine that real knowledge comes only from what you can observe and measure.

    4. That is the view that a theory is a useful tool and never a claim about what is really there, which is called instrumentalism, and it is the opposite of realism.

    One of these is the ordinary English sense of the word and the trap in the question.

  17. Social construction · the sociology of knowledge

    17 How does something become a real social institution, on the account given?

    1. Law is one way and the account given is more general.

    2. The conditions listed: knowledge of a thing encourages behaviour that increases other people's tendency to act as though it exists, there is reasonably common knowledge of it, and that knowledge is transmitted. Politeness is a good test case, since there is no law requiring it and no building that houses it, and it still constrains everybody.

    3. The account is explicitly about things that are not given by nature.

    4. No agreement is required on this account, only behaviour.

    Her example was something with no law behind it that everyone still obeys.

  18. Social construction · why gender counts as real

    18 The passage she quoted says gender is real. On what grounds?

    1. The passage says genders are undoubtedly not simply given by nature.

    2. Not the argument given.

    3. The test offered for reality is causal power: a thing is real if it makes a difference to what happens. The loop in the middle is the mechanism, since treating people as gendered produces gendered people, which then justifies the treatment. Historical research and differences between cultures show it is not simply given by nature.

    4. The passage appeals to divergences between cultures, which is the opposite.

    The passage names one test for whether something is real, and it is about effects.

  19. Social construction · contingently real

    19 Knowledge, methods, epistemologies, disciplinary boundaries and styles of work are called real social objects, and contingently real. What does contingently real mean?

    1. A view about what counts as knowledge and how you are allowed to get it is an epistemology, and the list in the question is a list of things like that. Contingent means it could have been otherwise. The boundary between chemistry and physics is real, since it decides who gets hired into which department and whose paper gets reviewed by whom, and nothing in nature required the line to fall where it does.

    2. Being real for some people and not others is a different claim, and these objects constrain everyone in the field.

    3. Disciplinary boundaries are not the kind of thing that gets disproved.

    4. The word real in the phrase is meant seriously, which is the point of it.

    Contingent is the opposite of necessary. Ask whether it had to be this way.

  20. Social construction · the laboratory

    20 Karin Knorr Cetina wrote that nature is not to be found in the laboratory. What did she mean?

    1. A charge of fraud, and not her claim.

    2. The materials in a laboratory are real. The point is what has been done to them.

    3. Think of what is actually on a bench: purified reagents, bred strains, filtered air, controlled temperature. Every one of those is nature with most of nature removed. So a result from a laboratory is a result about a highly artificial arrangement, and getting it to apply outside is extra work and never automatic.

    4. Not an argument about scale.

    Picture what is physically on a laboratory bench, and ask how much of it you could find outdoors.

  21. Social construction · how theories get built

    21 What is meant by saying scientific theories are constructed with reference to data but are not implied by the data?

    1. The claim holds even when the data are perfect.

    2. Kevin's own gloss in his notes says it plainly: a theory is based on real observations, and the observations alone do not automatically tell you exactly what theory to create. Her image was geometrical, making patterns appear given the fixed points that practice produces, the way more than one curve passes through the same scatter of dots.

    3. The order described runs from data to representation.

    4. A real position in the field, and a different claim from the one made here.

    Picture a handful of dots on a page and ask how many curves pass through all of them.

  22. Social construction · heterogeneous construction

    22 What is heterogeneous engineering, in Law's sense?

    1. Heterogeneous here means mixing kinds of thing, and not mixing branches of engineering.

    2. Neither half can usually be done on its own. The network is heterogeneous because it joins isolated parts of the material and the social world: equipment, established knowledge, patrons, money, institutions. And no single piece determines the shape of the whole, which is why a better device does not win by being better.

    3. The opposite of the claim, which is that the environment has to be built too.

    4. Not the sense of the term.

    The word heterogeneous points at the mixture of material things and social things in one network.

  23. Social construction · co-production

    23 Science and social order are said to be co-produced. What does that mean, and what was the example?

    1. A one-way claim, and co-production runs both ways.

    2. Both directions at once. Researchers need funding to study climate change, so the policy produces the science, and the science is then used to justify the policy. The ADHD case shows the same loop in a classification: the category decides who counts as ill, and who counts as ill is then evidence that the category tracks something.

    3. Replication, and a different idea.

    4. Not the sense of co-production used here.

    The prefix co means the causation runs in both directions at once.

  24. The construction of kinds

    24 On the construction of kinds, what separates the nominalist from the realist?

    1. Both sides think classification works. They disagree about why.

    2. They are answering one question, about whether the categories were found or made.

    3. Kevin flagged this one as examinable while it was being taught. The nominalist's hard problem is that we agree on classifications so easily, which looks like evidence of tracking something real; the answer is that shared training explains the agreement. Behind it is Hacking: another theory could have been established, so success is not proof.

    4. The overstatement. The claim is about our categories and leaves alone the question of whether there is anything out there.

    Both camps agree that classifying works. The disagreement is about what makes it work.

    2. The second half is the one that answers the week. After a lecture arguing that laboratories exclude nature and that facts are constructed, she ends by saying those same laboratories produce objectivity. The construction claim is about how objective knowledge gets made, and not a denial that it exists.

    3. The lecture spends its length arguing that truth is not what explains the power here.

    4. A point from the first half of the week, and not this conclusion.

    Her last line gives laboratories a positive role, which is a surprise after an hour of construction talk.
