Cheat sheet

Two things only, as asked: who argued what and in what order, then every term the course uses with a plain definition.

The map

Eleven stops in order. Each one gives the question the person was answering, what they actually claimed, and who they were arguing with. The line between two cards says how the first leads to the second. The three cards with a green edge are the modern writers who look back at everything above them and say what it was really doing.

1

Plato

428 to 348 BCE

What is actually real, and how would we find out?

His claim
The real things are the Forms, perfect originals in a realm outside this one. Everything you can see is a copy. The senses show you shadows on a cave wall, so they cannot be the route to knowledge.
The method
Dialectic: question and answer until opinion has been separated from knowledge.
Education
Not pouring knowledge into an empty soul. Turning the soul around so it faces the light. The eyes already work; they are pointed the wrong way.
Politics
Whoever has turned should rule. The Kalliopolis, the beautiful city, run by philosopher kings educated in geometry and astronomy.

His own student keeps the word form and moves it out of that other realm.

2

Aristotle

384 to 322 BCE

What makes things move, and where do they come from?

His claim
Form is in the thing itself, and every thing also has a telos, an end it is growing toward. An acorn is heading for oak. Know the end and you know the thing.
Consequence
Because form is here in the world, you can go and cut animals open and learn something. She called him the first biologist. Plato's theory points away from the physical world and produces no zoology.
Against
Plato's separate realm of Forms.
Attacked later by
Bacon, over telos. Copernicus, over his earth-centred cosmos. Kuhn uses telos as the thing foundationalism is not like.

His cosmos then holds for two thousand years, which is what makes the next break so large.

3

The Middle Ages

read by Lynn White Jr.

Why did Western Europe build the technology it built?

His claim
The religious picture drives the technology, and not the other way round.
The chain
God is a creator and an architect. Man is made in his image and told to rule the world as a creative cooperator. Christian history runs in one direction toward a goal, so there is no time to lose. Therefore manual work becomes a form of worship.
By 1300
Deism: God as a clockmaker who set the world going and stepped back. Once the world is a mechanism, taking it apart to see how it runs becomes a pious act.
Against
The view that economic need drives technology and religion follows behind.

That is the setting. Now the Greek picture itself starts to come apart, first in its content.

4

Copernicus

1473 to 1543

How do the planets actually move?

His claim
The sun stands still at the centre and the earth moves around it.
Her framing, and she called it the important one
He put the sun at the centre so that circular motion would work. He was protecting an old Greek commitment to perfect circles. A revolution launched in order to save something ancient, and not because new data forced it.
Against
Aristotle's earth-centred cosmos, which was also the Church's.
Used later by
Kuhn, as his worked case of a revolution.

The content of the Greek picture has broken. Next the method breaks.

5

Bacon

1561 to 1626 · first of two appearances

How should anyone find anything out?

His claim
Empiricism: the senses are the original source of knowledge. And you do not wait for nature to reveal itself. You go and make it answer, by experiment.
The slogan
Knowledge is power. He means power over the physical world, and he means it as a promise. It is the advertisement for the new science.
Ant, spider, bee
The alchemists are ants: they collect and never think. The Aristotelians are spiders: they spin systems out of their own entrails and take nothing in. The proper natural philosopher is a bee: gathers, then digests.
Against
Aristotle's telos. If every thing already contains its purpose, the world reveals itself to a patient observer and your job is to receive it. Bacon wants you to go and take it.
His expectation
That a method anyone can follow will level the differences in intellectual ability, so genius becomes unnecessary. Week 4 tests this against the real distribution of output, and it fails.

Not everyone is pleased about the new confidence in progress.

6

Rousseau

1712 to 1778

Has progress in the sciences and arts actually made us better people?

His claim
No. A return to Greek values is what society and education need, and progress is possible only through education.
His line
Barbarous people are those who lack virtue, which she tied back to Plato, Socrates and Aristotle.
Why he is here
He is the counter-current inside the Enlightenment: a man of the age of progress doubting the thing the age was proudest of.

Meanwhile the practical question of how to run institutions is being answered by arithmetic.

7

Bentham

1748 to 1832

How should an institution be arranged?

His claim
Utilitarianism: the right action is whichever produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. That is what he was famous for in his lifetime.
Applied
He spent much of his life on prison reform and designed the panopticon as the humane replacement for hanging, transportation and filthy gaols. It was never built in Britain.
The design
A ring of cells round a central tower. Two windows per cell so light crosses it and the occupant shows as a silhouette. His three words: visibility, unverifiability, and external power becoming non-corporeal, meaning it stops needing a body in the tower.
Read against him later by
Foucault, who takes the humane design and shows what it does.

Bacon's confidence in method, meanwhile, hardens into a doctrine about what counts as knowledge at all.

8

Positivism

Enlightenment onward, Comte and the Vienna Circle

What counts as real knowledge?

The claim
Only what you can observe and measure. Positive here has nothing to do with good.
Her description
Research that is non-contextual, meaning where and when you did it should not matter; formal and standardised, meaning anyone following the procedure gets the same result; and analytic, meaning it separates distinct variables and holds the rest still.
Attacked by
Kuhn, and the first of those three is the one he destroys.

Which brings the course to its hinge.

9

Kuhn

1922 to 1996 · The Structure of Scientific Revolutions

Does science really accumulate steadily toward the truth?

His stand
No. Science alternates between long stable stretches and revisionary breaks. He is against positivism and against the steady-progress story.
His pieces
  • Paradigm. The whole shared kit a field works inside: fixed theories, values, instruments, and assumptions about what exists. Her examples: Newton's mechanics, Lavoisier's chemistry, Mendel's genetics.
  • Normal science. Puzzle solving inside that kit. A puzzle has a known shape and a guaranteed answer, so failing one means you failed, never that the paradigm did.
  • Anomaly. A result the field cannot make fit however hard it tries. They pile up.
  • Revolution. The kit gets revised and the anomalous problem gets solved.
  • Incommensurability. Afterwards there may be no shared measure for comparing the old kit with the new. Three reasons: the meanings of terms change, indoctrination, and historical context changes.
On history
He rejects Whig history, which builds the past into steps leading to us. Events must follow their causes, so a sequence cannot be explained by the fact that it ended up here.
On progress
Normal science visibly accumulates. A revolution is not progress, because it builds and destroys paradigms at the same time, so there is no single direction to measure.
Her conclusion
Science is ad hoc and disunified, held together the way Wittgenstein said games are, and it works anyway. So the course's title question has an answer: there is no one scientific method.

If the shape of a science can shift like that, the next question is who benefits from the shape it has.

10

Foucault

1926 to 1984

What are modern institutions actually doing to people?

History of Madness, 1961
The claimed scientific neutrality of modern psychiatry covers for controlling challenges to conventional bourgeois morality. Her examples: homosexuality and hysteria, both diagnoses attached to broken social rules.
Discipline and Punish, 1975
A genealogy, meaning a study of how a practice came to be without assuming it was an improvement. His puzzle: punishment really did get gentler, so what is the gentler method doing that the old one could not?
Three techniques
Hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, the examination. Watch, rank, record. The examination is where the first two meet: it watches you and writes a verdict about you in one act.
The goal
Normalization. A norm is set and everyone is measured against it, which produces systems sorting citizens into normal and abnormal. Her examples are all places nobody is being punished: standardized education, medical practice, industrial production.
Bacon, second appearance
Power-knowledge. Foucault keeps Bacon's equation and changes two things. Bacon's power is over nature; this power is over people. Bacon is making a promise; this is an accusation. His gloss: the development of force and the establishment of truth, one act with two outputs. A file written about a prisoner becomes the truth about him and decides whether he goes home.
Docile bodies
Discipline makes bodies useful and easier to control: prisoners, soldiers, workers, schoolchildren. The body is broken into segments, analysed, and recomposed for maximum effect.

Kuhn says the content of science could have gone differently. Foucault says knowing has a politics. Put those together and you get the last stop.

11

Social construction

Sismondo, and Hacking 1999

In what sense is a scientific fact made?

Hacking's claim
Any given theory is not the only one that could have been established, so the success of a theory is not by itself evidence for it. It could have gone another way and still worked.
How something gets to be real
Institutions exist because enough people act as though they exist, so they cannot be wished away. Her example is politeness: no law requires it, no building houses it, and it constrains everybody.
The test for reality
Causal power. Gender is real because it is hard not to take account of it, and because treating people as gendered produces gendered people. Having effects is the best sign of reality we have.
Contingently real
Knowledge, methods, epistemologies, disciplinary boundaries and styles of work are all real and none of them had to be this way.
Kinds
Nominalists: kinds are human impositions, so the objects of classification are mental and linguistic. Realists: kinds are real features of the world. Her test case was ADHD.
The sting in the tail
After an hour arguing that laboratories shut nature out, she ends by saying laboratories and technology contribute to objectivity. The construction claim is about how objective knowledge gets made, and not a denial that it exists.

Who turns up twice

Three names appear in more than one week, in different roles. These are the ones that catch people out, because the same person is a hero in one lecture and a target in the next.

Baconthree times
Week 2, as the founder. Empiricism, experiment, and knowledge is power as a promise about nature. Week 3, inside Foucault. The same three words become power-knowledge: same equation, power now over people, and the verdict reversed from boast to charge. Week 4, as a prediction that failed. He expected method to level intellectual ability. Ten per cent of authors produce half of all papers.
Aristotlethree times
Week 2, in his own right. Form in the thing, plus telos. Week 2 again, as Bacon's target. Telos is precisely what Bacon objects to. Week 3, as the cosmos Copernicus overthrows, and as the contrast Kuhn draws for foundationalism: telos pulls from ahead, a foundation pushes from below.
Platotwice
Week 2, for the cave and for education as turning around. Week 3, beside Foucault, because she wrote on her own slide that his panoptic utopia should make you think of Plato, the Republic, the Kalliopolis. One perfectly ordered city described twice, once admiringly and once as a warning.

General words

These belong to no single thinker and turn up all term.

Ad hoc
Put together for the particular case in hand, with no general rule behind it. Why it matters here. Her conclusion about science was that it is an ad hoc system, disunified, and successful anyway. She attached that to Wittgenstein, and his example is games. Ask what all games have in common. Competition? Solitaire has none. Winning and losing? Ring-a-ring-a-roses has none. Skill? Snakes and ladders is pure luck. Two players? Solitaire again. Fun? Not professional sport. There is no single feature present in every game. What there is instead is a web of overlaps: chess and poker share competition, poker and solitaire share cards, solitaire and patience share a single player. He called that a family resemblance, because relatives look alike without any one feature running through all of them. Her claim is that science is like that: physics, taxonomy, epidemiology and geology share overlapping features and no one defining method, and the whole thing works. So the course's title question gets a real answer: there is no scientific method, singular, and that is survivable.
Contingent
It could have been otherwise. Nothing made it necessary. Contingently real means a thing is genuinely real and still could have been built differently, which is how week 4 describes disciplinary boundaries.
Metaphysics
The study of what exists and what the things in the cosmos fundamentally are. The name is a filing accident: compilers put Aristotle's book after his Physics.
Dualism
A split of one thing into two. Schadewaldt's complaint is that modern thought split nature from spirit, thought from extension, and subject from object, where the Greeks had held them together.

Greek terms

physisSchadewaldt
Nature, meaning the creative origin that everything grows out of. It implies movement, which is why it answers the Greek question about what makes things move.
techneSchadewaldt
Skill at making or doing something. Produced by people, which makes it a combination of man and nature. On its own it is no part of nature.
epistemeSchadewaldt
Theoretical knowledge, of what is immutable and primary. Ranked above techne, which deals with what is still changing.
empeiriaSchadewaldt
Plain experience, know-how with no account behind it. Techne belongs between this and episteme.
atechniaSchadewaldt
Productive knowledge built on a false idea of the thing itself, held confidently. His word for a blunder. It can be rich and diverse and still be one.
telosAristotle
The end or goal of a thing. Everything has a purpose it is growing toward, and knowing a thing means knowing its end. The root of the English teleology.
Form
Plato: the Forms are perfect originals in a realm outside this one, and things here are copies. Aristotle: form is in the thing itself, together with its telos. That is why Aristotle could do biology.
DialecticPlato
His method of inquiry, and the best route to truth: separating opinion from knowledge and understanding.
KalliopolisPlato
The beautiful city. His ideal state, ruled by philosophers, where correct education is central. She paired it with Foucault's perfectly governed city.
Philosopher kingsPlato
The rulers and guardians of the Kalliopolis. Awake while everyone else is dreaming. Their education needs geometry and astronomy.

Method and knowledge

EmpiricismBacon
The senses are the original source of knowledge. From the Greek empeiria, experience. Its opposite is the view that reason alone gets you there, which is Plato.
Induction
Reasoning upward from particular cases to a general law. A thousand white swans, so all swans are white. The conclusion covers every swan there is and you checked a thousand, so the cases never make the law safe. Europeans held that one until 1697, when a Dutch expedition found black swans in Western Australia.
Foundationalism
Every claim rests on a lower claim, down to a bottom layer that needs no reason under it. Why will the kettle boil at 100? Because water does. Why believe that? Because every measurement came out that way. There the chain is meant to stop. The problem is that the bottom layer is laid by induction. The tracing runs downward through reasons, and never backward through time, which is Whig history and a separate idea.
Knowledge is powerBacon
Know how a thing works and you can make it do what you want. He meant power over the physical world and he meant it as a promise.
Ant, spider, beeBacon
Ant: the alchemists, who collect and never think. Spider: the Aristotelians, spinning systems out of their own entrails. Bee: the proper natural philosopher, who gathers and then digests. Novum Organum I.95.
DeismLynn White
God as a clockmaker who set the world going and stepped back. Developing around 1300. It makes studying the mechanism a pious act.
Positivism
Real knowledge comes only from what you can observe and measure. As she described it: non-contextual, formal and standardised research that separates distinct variables. Positive here has nothing to do with good.
Realism
Truths depend mostly on the natural world and only slightly on the people stating them. There is a way the world is, it can be discovered, and it can be represented accurately.
Instrumentalism
A theory is a useful tool and never a claim about what is really there. The opposite of realism.
Epistemology
A view about what counts as knowledge and how you are allowed to get it.

Kuhn

Normal science
Puzzle solving inside a shared paradigm, during the long stable stretches between revolutions. A puzzle has a known shape and a guaranteed answer, so failing one means you failed, never that the paradigm did.
Paradigm
A pattern for others to follow, working as a theoretical and methodological tool. The whole shared kit: fixed theories, values, instruments and assumptions about what exists. Her examples: Newton's mechanics, Lavoisier's chemistry, Mendel's genetics.
Disciplinary matrix
Kuhn's later and more careful word for a paradigm.
Anomaly
A result a field cannot make fit, however hard it tries. A pile of them is what eventually breaks a paradigm.
Revolution
The disciplinary matrix is revised and an anomalous problem gets solved. Not progress, because a revolution builds and destroys paradigms at the same time.
Incommensurability
No common measure for comparing two paradigms. It does not mean you can say nothing about both. It means there is no shared unit to state the comparison in.

Where the word comes from. Greek geometry. Two lengths are incommensurable when no single unit divides both exactly. Give a square a side of 1 and its diagonal is the square root of 2, which runs 1.41421356 and onward with digits that never end and never repeat. Choose any unit you like, a tenth, a thousandth, a billionth of the side: the side is a whole number of them and the diagonal never is. A ruler gives you an approximation of both. What does not exist is one unit that fits both exactly.

Planet. For Ptolemy the sun and the moon are planets and the earth is not. For Copernicus the earth is a planet and the sun is not. So "how many planets are there" has no shared answer, because the word picks out a different set of objects in each system.

Mass. In Newton, mass is a fixed property of an object, the same whatever the object is doing. In Einstein it changes with velocity. "Mass is conserved" is true in one and false in the other, and the two sentences are not even making the same claim.

Burning. Phlogiston chemistry says a burning metal loses something. Lavoisier says it gains oxygen. Each theory also set itself a different list of questions it had to answer, so "which explains more" depends on whose list you count.

Her three reasons: the meanings of terms change, indoctrination, and historical context changes. Her counter-example: particle detectors stayed in use unchanged across a theory change, so something survived that both sides trust.
Whig history
History that builds the past into a series of steps toward present views. Kuhn's objection: events must follow their causes, so a sequence cannot be explained by the fact that it represents progress.
Copernicus1473 to 1543
Sun stationary, earth moving, against Aristotle's earth-centred cosmos. Her framing: he held that the sun needed to be at the centre for circular motion to work.

Foucault

PanopticonBentham
A ring of cells round a central tower, each cell with two windows so light crosses it and the occupant shows as a silhouette. The inmate is permanently visible and can never check whether the tower is occupied.
Bentham's three words
Visibility, unverifiability, and external power becoming non-corporeal. Non-corporeal means the power stops needing a body in the tower.
UtilitarianismBentham
The right action is whichever produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. What he was actually famous for, ahead of the panopticon.
Three techniques of control
Hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, and the examination. Watch, rank, record. The examination is where the first two meet.
Power-knowledge
Power and knowledge as one thing with the same goal, which is controlling what we know. His gloss: the development of force and the establishment of truth. He builds it on Bacon's knowledge is power and turns a promise into an accusation.
Normalization
The goal of disciplinary power. It produces judicial systems sorting citizens into normal and abnormal. Her examples: standardized education, medical practices, industrial processes.
Docile bodies
Bodies made useful and easier to control by discipline: prisoners, soldiers, workers, schoolchildren. The body becomes a machine, broken into segments, analysed and recomposed for maximum effect.
Genealogy
Tracing how a practice came to be without assuming it was an improvement. The method of Discipline and Punish, 1975.
The plague town
How Panopticism opens. The plague is met with Order: sorting the disease, separating bodies, sorting evil, which increases fear and death. Its political dream is strict division and regulation reaching the smallest details of daily life.
History of Madness1961
The claimed scientific neutrality of modern treatment of insanity covers for controlling challenges to conventional bourgeois morality. Her examples: homosexuality and hysteria.

Week 4

Stratification
The layering of a field into a small advantaged group and everyone else. 10 per cent of authors produce half of all papers; 80 per cent of citations go to 20 per cent of papers.
Cumulative advantage
Success breeds success. A good first post means a bigger lab, more results, a stronger grant, a bigger lab again. It makes scientific success look elitist where it had looked merit based.
Meritocracy
A system where who gets ahead tracks how good they are. Her conclusion was that science looks much more like a boys' club than one of these.
Zuckerman 1977
34 of the 55 US Nobel laureates in 1963 had studied or collaborated with a previous winner. Those who had won at an average age of 44, against 53 for the others.
Rossiter
Feminisation lowers a field's status, masculinisation raises it. Women were pushed into the invisible sciences such as home economics and nutritional science.
Wenneras and Wold 2001
At the Swedish Medical Research Council a woman needed about three extra papers in Nature or Science, or 20 extra in a good specialist journal, to be scored as competent as a man.
Social construction
The construction of facts, knowledge, theories, phenomena, science, technologies and societies. Truth, rationality and the material world have limited value without interpretation or rhetorical work.
ConstructivismHacking 1999
Our categories are human handiwork. Any given theory is not the only one that could have been established, so a theory's success is not by itself evidence for it.
Contingently real
Real, and it did not have to be this way. Knowledge, methods, epistemologies, disciplinary boundaries and styles of work are all real social objects of this kind.
Nominalist and realist about kinds
Nominalist: kinds are human impositions, so the objects of classification are mental and linguistic. Realist: kinds are real features of the world. Her test case was ADHD.
Knorr Cetina
Nature is not to be found in the laboratory. To an outside observer it is a site of action from which nature is as much as possible excluded: a space of prepared materials, intentionally shaped.
Heterogeneous engineeringLaw 1987
Building the artifact and the environment it can work in at the same time, by enrolling materials, skills, knowledge, capital and people. No one piece of the network determines the shape of the whole.
Co-production
Scientific knowledge and social arrangements make each other. Researchers need funding to study climate change, and the science then justifies the policy. ADHD can be framed as a disorder or as extreme procrastination.
