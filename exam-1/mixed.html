  1. The Greeks · before Plato

    1 Homer, eighth century BCE, is the earliest writer this term reaches back to. What picture of the world does the Odyssey rely on?

    1. Eighth century BCE, and it sets up the background assumption everything later argues inside: there is an order out there. Heraclitus then says nature is in harmony, Plato puts the order in a realm outside ours, and Aristotle puts it in the things themselves.

    2. The opposite of the picture she drew from it.

    3. Nothing in the lecture claims that.

    4. That split is Schadewaldt's subject, and he says the Greeks did not have it.

    He is in the course to supply the assumption the Greeks all share before anyone starts arguing.

  2. Plato · what happens to the man who goes back

    2 In the cave, a prisoner gets free, goes up, sees the sun, and comes back down. What do the others do?

    1. If leaving were that easy there would be no allegory.

    2. Two separate reactions and they are easy to run together. Ridicule is for the returner. The killing is reserved for whoever tries to unchain them. Plato is writing not long after Athens executed Socrates, which is the obvious thing the second half is pointing at.

    3. The common misremembering. The ridicule falls on the returner; the threat of killing attaches to the act of unchaining.

    4. Too mild for the text, which gives them both a reaction and a threat.

    Two different reactions, aimed at two different things he might do.

  3. Plato against Bacon · what mathematics is for

    3 Both Plato and the new science of the 1600s put mathematics near the centre. They want different things from it. What is the difference?

    1. She drew one explicitly, in the Bacon lecture.

    2. The right contrast with the two sides swapped.

    3. Which is why Plato wants his philosopher kings taught geometry and astronomy: the subjects deal in things you cannot touch, so they turn the soul away from the visible world. The new science wants the opposite, a number you can check against the sky tomorrow night.

    4. He requires it in the education of his rulers.

    • Dialectic: question and answer until opinion has been sorted out from knowledge

    One of them wants mathematics to point away from the visible world. The other wants it to point at tomorrow night's sky.

  4. Schadewaldt · whose dualism

    4 Schadewaldt says modern thought developed a dualism between thought and extension. Which philosopher is that split usually credited to?

    1. Not a figure in these lectures.

    2. Bacon's split is between the knower and nature as something to be forced to answer, and the thought-and-extension formula is not his.

    3. His form and matter sit together in one object, so no such split.

    4. Thinking substance against extended substance: mind on one side, everything that takes up space on the other. Once the observer is made of one kind of thing and the world of another, the observer is standing outside what is observed, which is the arrangement Schadewaldt wants undone.

    • Dualism: a split of one thing into two

    Thinking substance on one side, and everything that takes up space on the other.

  5. Comte · the three states

    5 Comte appears in the week 2 packet with a claim about how explanation changes over history. What is the sequence?

    1. Gods explain it, then abstract essences and forces explain it, then only what can be observed and measured counts. Holding that only the observable and measurable counts is the doctrine called positivism, and the name of that third stage is where the word comes from. Note that this is a progress story, which puts Comte on the opposite side from Kuhn.

    2. Not his terms.

    3. A rough chronology of the course and not Comte's three states.

    4. A description of method. The question asks for a sequence of historical stages.

    • Metaphysics: the study of what exists, and of what things fundamentally are

    The name of the third stage is the name of a doctrine Kuhn later attacks.

  6. The Vienna Circle

    6 What was the Vienna Circle?

    1. Not a group in this course.

    2. Logical positivism, with Moritz Schlick at the centre. It is the hardest and most organised version of the doctrine Kuhn's book breaks with, which is why she introduced Kuhn as marking a break with positive doctrines.

    3. Four centuries too early and the wrong city.

    4. Different century, different argument.

    Their test for whether a sentence means anything is the same test positivism applies to knowledge.

  7. Galileo · her contrast with Bacon

    7 Bacon objects that the Aristotelian waits for the world to reveal its built-in purposes. Galileo is the standing counter-example. What did he do that makes him one?

    1. That is Bacon's contribution in this course.

    2. A real episode. She used him for something else here.

    3. Bacon's complaint about Aristotle is that the world is supposed to reveal itself to a patient observer who grasps its built-in purposes. Galileo, 1564 to 1642, is the counter-example: he pointed an instrument at the sky and found out. He went and got the answer. Nobody waited for it to arrive.

    4. His findings cut against the Aristotelian cosmos.

    • Induction: reasoning up from the cases you have seen to a law covering all of them

    Bacon says the Aristotelian waits for the world to reveal itself. She needed somebody who did the other thing.

  8. Lynn White · why the shape of history matters

    8 Lynn White says Christian history is unique and unilinear, accelerating toward a spiritual goal. Why does that detail carry his argument?

    1. A different episode, in a different century.

    2. His arrow runs the other way.

    3. Age is not part of his argument.

    4. Compare it with a cyclical history, where the world returns to where it started and nothing is gained by hurrying. A straight line with an end point turns labour into something owed. That is how he gets from a doctrine about time to a watermill.

    Compare it with a history that goes round in a circle. What changes about the value of hurrying?

  9. Across weeks · the same three words, twice

    9 Bacon says knowledge is power. Foucault says power-knowledge. What stayed the same and what changed?

    1. Her notes say directly that Foucault builds on Bacon.

    2. The verdict flips, from a promise into a charge.

    3. Bacon's power is over the physical world and he is advertising it. Foucault's is over people and he is accusing. Her notes say Foucault builds on Bacon, so the echo is deliberate. Taking a famous optimistic slogan and turning it into a threat is how he makes the point land.

    4. Foucault means it literally too: the written record about a person is what decides what happens to them.

    • Power-knowledge: knowing someone and controlling them turn out to be a single act

    One part of it is the same claim in both mouths. Two parts are different.

  10. Across weeks · two perfectly ordered cities

    10 Plato describes the Kalliopolis. Foucault describes the plague town and the panoptic utopia. Both are cities where order reaches into everything. What separates them?

    1. Education is central to both, which is part of why she paired them.

    2. The plague town is historical and the panopticon was never built, so the line does not fall there. And she put the two side by side on purpose.

    3. She wrote on her own slide, beside the panoptic utopia, think Plato, Republic, the Kalliopolis. The same dream described twice, once as an ideal and once as a diagnosis. Which means Foucault's target is older than the nineteenth century.

    4. Plato's city is run by philosopher kings.

    • Kalliopolis: his beautiful city, run by philosopher kings, where the right education is what holds it together

    The arrangement is close to the same in both. The attitude toward it is where they part.

  11. Across weeks · who doubts progress

    11 Three figures in this course doubt that things are getting better. Which three, and on what?

    1. All three are on the progress side.

    2. None of them is making a claim about progress in this course.

    3. None of the three makes that argument in these lectures.

    4. Three different doubts about three different things. Rousseau's is moral, Kuhn's is about the structure of knowledge, and Foucault's is about power. Worth holding as a set, because a question asking which thinker resists the progress story could land on any of them.

    One doubt is about morals, one about knowledge, one about power.

  12. Kuhn · his examples

    12 Kuhn names three worked examples of a paradigm. Which three?

    1. All three are in the course and this is not the trio she listed.

    2. Each one gave a later generation a complete working kit: what exists, what counts as a question, which instruments to use. Lavoisier's ideas about elements and the conservation of mass became the frame later chemists worked inside without having to argue about it.

    3. Whole disciplines, and a paradigm is narrower than a discipline.

    4. Three abandoned theories, which would make the point only about failures.

    • Paradigm: the shared picture a field works from, with the instruments, standards and assumptions that come with it

    Three named people, each from a different science, each leaving behind a working kit.

  13. Kuhn · the logic behind rejecting Whig history

    13 Kuhn says a sequence of events cannot be explained by the fact that it represents progress. What is his reason?

    1. An explanation says what made something happen, and what made it happen has to come first. Saying Copernicus moved the sun because it led to modern astronomy puts the cause three centuries after the effect. Tidy to say and impossible as an explanation.

    2. Disagreement among historians is not the problem he names.

    3. He grants that normal science visibly accumulates.

    4. An argument he could have made. The one she quoted is about logic.

    Ask when a cause has to happen, relative to the thing it causes.

  14. Kuhn · one word, two meanings

    14 Newton and Einstein both use the word mass. Why does that make the two theories hard to compare?

    1. Both are precise. They are precise about different things.

    2. Mass is conserved comes out true in one theory and false in the other, and the two sentences are not even disagreeing, because they are about different quantities. This is the first of Kuhn's three reasons for incommensurability, the one about meanings of terms changing, and it is the reason that gets attacked hardest.

    3. Newtonian mechanics is still used daily, so the relation is more awkward than a refutation.

    4. Not a translation problem between languages.

    Ask whether the quantity the word names stays put when the object starts moving fast.

  15. Foucault · what the watching produces

    15 Hierarchical observation produces something Foucault calls a unified whole. What is it, and how does he gloss it?

    1. One act with two outputs at the same moment: force grows and a truth gets fixed. That is why he writes it with a hyphen. Two words joined by and would be two things that cooperate; he means one thing with two faces.

    2. Both terms are real and neither is what hierarchical observation is said to produce.

    3. The examination is one of the three techniques, alongside hierarchical observation.

    4. Not the phrase she quoted.

    • Three techniques of control: hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, and the examination
    • Power-knowledge: knowing someone and controlling them turn out to be a single act
    • Normalization: a norm gets set and everyone is measured against it, so people sort into normal and abnormal
    • Docile bodies: bodies made useful and easier to control by discipline

    His gloss has two halves joined by and, one about force and one about truth.

  16. Foucault · which technique does two jobs

    16 Of the three techniques of control, one is where the other two meet. Which, and how?

    1. The three are described as fitting together, with one of them doing the joining.

    2. Ranks watch. The judging against a norm is a separate technique.

    3. It uses the watching and it does not perform it.

    4. A school exam is the clean case. It observes your performance and produces a permanent record that then follows you and decides what you are allowed to do next. Watch, rank, record, with the third doing the first two at once.

    • Three techniques of control: hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, and the examination
    • Normalization: a norm gets set and everyone is measured against it, so people sort into normal and abnormal

    Think about what a school exam physically does to you in one sitting.

  17. Week 4 · the portrait of a productive scientist

    17 One survey built a picture of the most productive scientist. Which detail was on the list?

    1. Prestige of graduate school, mentor and employer are all on the list as positives.

    2. Two of these are the reverse of the survey's findings.

    3. Fox 1983. The list runs long and includes being driven, having a strong ego, tolerating ambiguity, being at home with abstractions, being relatively young, and having a prestigious mentor and a prestigious employer. Notice how much of it describes a situation and how little describes a person. That is why she printed the whole list.

    4. The survey says detached from non-scientific social relations.

    Half the list describes a personality and half describes a situation. The right answer has both kinds in it.

  18. Week 4 · the numbers on women in science

    18 In the United States in 2005 and 2006, what did the figures show?

    1. Far below the figures given, which reach 46 and 49 per cent in some fields.

    2. The figure is 19 per cent.

    3. 18 per cent against 49 per cent is the contrast she drew.

    4. 49 per cent of biological science doctorates, 18 per cent in engineering, and in 2006 only 19 per cent of senior faculty across these fields, concentrated in the life and social sciences. The gap between the intake figure and the senior figure is the pipeline leaking. Her aside: the humanities are doing better, apart from philosophy.

    Two of the figures are close to half and one is close to a fifth. The fifth turns up twice.

  19. Week 4 · the advertisements

    19 An analysis of advertisements in the journal Science found a pattern in the words. What was it?

    1. Barbercheck 2001. Men were also more likely to be shown as scientific heroes, as nonconformists, and at play. Worth having because it is the cheapest possible evidence: nobody intended any of it, and it is sitting in the trade journal of the field.

    2. Not the finding.

    3. The study is about how people in them were depicted.

    4. The study found a difference, and reported it as cultural content nobody would care to acknowledge.

    One set of words is about being undemanding. The other is about performance.

  20. Week 4 · the drying agent

    20 A female doctoral student is quoted saying her colleagues went to parties with her but never asked her which drying agent to use. What does the quotation illustrate?

    1. No policy is involved, which is the point.

    2. Her training is the same; the asking is what differs.

    3. Her account describes friendly colleagues, and the usefulness of the quotation turns on that.

    4. Etzkowitz and colleagues, 2000. Nobody refused her anything. It simply never crossed their minds to ask her a technical question, so she was never part of the exchange that makes somebody a colleague. That is the kind of difference each instance of which looks trivial and which compounds.

    Notice that everyone in the story is being perfectly friendly.

  21. Week 4 · do women learn differently

    21 Some feminists and non-feminists have argued that men and women tend to think differently. What was the claim, and who made it?

    1. Rossiter's argument is that women were pushed into them.

    2. The Harvard episode, and a different claim from the one about learning patterns.

    3. Gilligan 1982 and Keller 1985. Hold it apart from the maths gene claim, which the lecture rejects outright because genes only act in environments. This one is treated as a live proposal about learning styles and it comes from inside feminism as well as outside it.

    4. That is the maths gene claim, which she rejected as an ideological statement, meaning one that states a political position while dressed as a finding.

    Two different claims about men and women appear in that lecture. One she rejects flatly, and one she reports as a live proposal.

  22. The course thesis

    22 The first-class slides give the course a thesis. What is it?

    1. Every week is a stage in that argument. Week 2 shows the method changing across two thousand years, week 3 shows it changing shape in a revolution, and week 4 says what it is made of. If an exam question asks what ties the course together, this is the sentence.

    2. Lynn White touches on it and it is not the thesis.

    3. Nothing in the course argues that, and week 4 ends by crediting laboratories with objectivity.

    4. The view the whole term is built against.

    One sentence, in two halves, and the whole term is an argument for it.

  23. Across weeks · putting ideas in order

    23 Four claims from the course. Which of them was made first?

    1. Foucault, 1975.

    2. Bacon, around 1600, roughly 375 years earlier. The order matters, because the later claim is built on the earlier one and reverses its verdict. Getting the order backwards makes the relationship impossible to state.

    3. Kuhn, 1962.

    4. Foucault again.

    • Incommensurability: no shared unit for measuring two paradigms against each other
    • Paradigm: the shared picture a field works from, with the instruments, standards and assumptions that come with it

    Three of these four belong to the twentieth century.

  24. Across weeks · telos, three times

    24 Telos turns up in three different arguments in this course. Which set is right?

    1. Aristotle: everything has an end it grows toward. Bacon: that makes the world something you wait to receive, so he wants experiment instead. Kuhn: a foundation pushes up from below, while telos pulls from ahead. One Greek word doing work in three centuries that are two thousand years apart.

    2. It is Aristotle's term, and Foucault does not use it.

    3. None of those three owns the term.

    4. She brought it back twice: in Bacon's objection, and in the Kuhn lecture on foundationalism.

    • Foundationalism: every claim rests on a claim below it, down to a bottom layer that needs nothing under it
    • telos: the end a thing is growing toward

    One man coins it, one man attacks it, and one man uses it to explain what his own idea is not.

  25. Across weeks · what the plague town and the panopticon share

    25 Foucault puts the plague town and Bentham's building in the same chapter. What do they have in common?

    1. The panopticon is a prison design.

    2. The plague town does it with an emergency as the excuse and the panopticon does it with architecture, permanently. That is his argument in one move: the emergency measure became the ordinary design. The plague's political dream is strict division and regulation reaching the smallest details, and the building delivers exactly that with nobody needing to be in the tower.

    3. The plague measures predate him by centuries.

    4. The plague measures are older and Bentham was English.

    One is a temporary emergency and one is a permanent building. Ask what they are both doing to the people inside.
