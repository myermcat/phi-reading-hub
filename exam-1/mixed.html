  1. Plato · the text, the author, the dialogue

    1 The allegory of the cave comes from which text, and who are the two speakers in it?

    1. The Apology is Socrates on trial. No cave in it.

    2. Book seven of the Republic. Glaucon is the one being walked through the cave. Worth holding all three together, because she said there is a question about the text, the author and the name of the dialogue: Plato wrote it, the dialogue is the Republic, and Plato never speaks in his own name anywhere in it.

    3. The Symposium is the drinking party about love.

    4. The Timaeus is Plato's account of how the cosmos was made.

    Plato wrote twenty-six dialogues. This one is his long work about the ideal city.

  2. Plato · what education is

    2 Plato writes that education is not "putting knowledge into souls that lack it, like putting sight into blind eyes". So what is it?

    1. This is the exact thing the quoted line rejects.

    2. That is skill at making or doing something, which the Greeks called techne, and Plato ranks it below the knowledge he is after here.

    3. Her phrase on the slide was "education is turning around". The eyes already work. They are pointed at the wall. Her line: sight is no good if you are not looking in the right place.

    4. Nothing in the lecture supports this, and it is the opposite of a method of inquiry.

    If the eyes are not broken, what is the problem with them?

  3. Plato and Foucault · why she put them side by side

    3 Beside Foucault's "panoptic utopia, the utopia of a perfectly governed city", the professor wrote on her own slide: "think Plato, Republic, the Kalliopolis". What is the Kalliopolis, and why is it there?

    1. Tempting, and this is the one to beware of. The Kalliopolis is a city, and the cave is a separate image.

    2. Plato's Kalliopolis is his own invention, and he is against Athenian democracy.

    3. Kalli means beautiful, so the most beautiful city. Her point in putting them together: Foucault says the plague town has a "political dream" of strict division and regulation reaching into the smallest details of everyday life, and that dream is old. Plato wrote the attractive version of it.

    4. The realm of the Forms is a transcendental realm, and it is not a city.

    Kalli is the Greek for beautiful. Polis is the Greek for city.

  4. Plato against Aristotle · which theory matches which philosopher

    4 Plato and Aristotle both use the word form. What is the difference?

    1. This is the right distinction with the names swapped, and it is the commonest way to lose this mark.

    2. For Plato the ideal forms are all in a realm outside this one, and the things around us are copies. Aristotle keeps the form in the dog and the tree, and adds the end it is heading for. That is why he can be called the first biologist and Plato cannot.

    3. Neither of them means shape, and neither means a definition.

    4. The disagreement is about where the form is, which is a disagreement about what exists.

    One of the two could do biology. Which one, and why does that follow from where he puts form?

  5. Aristotle · what form answers

    5 In the lecture, Aristotle's form and telos are offered as his answer to a question. Which question?

    1. That is the question put at the centre two thousand years later by Bacon and the empiricists, meaning the people who hold that knowledge starts in the senses.

    2. That is the Republic.

    3. This is the question the whole Greek picture is organised around, and the professor said so twice. Physis implies movement. Plato answers with the Forms in a realm outside ours. Aristotle answers with form in the thing and a goal it moves toward.

    4. That is Schadewaldt's question about physis and techne.

    Physis was defined in the lecture as implying one particular thing. What was it?

  6. Aristotle · what is critical to him

    6 Telos is the term she stressed as the one that is critical to Aristotle. What does it mean?

    1. The word literally means end. A dog, a tree and a person each have an end they are heading for, and knowing a thing means knowing that end. Remember it attached to Bacon, who hated it, and to Kuhn, who said the history of science cannot be explained by where it ended up.

    2. That is matter, which is the thing form is contrasted with.

    3. Closer to Plato's cave, and not a term from it.

    4. That is dialectic, and it is Plato's.

    The word survives in English in teleology.

  7. The era question that is really about the person

    7 Which of these is true of Aristotle?

    1. Every word of this is Plato. This is the trap, and she set it up in the lecture by giving both biographies together.

    2. Plato again.

    3. Aristotle is 384 to 322 BCE. Bacon is 1561 to 1626 CE, so this has him about two thousand years late.

  8. Schadewaldt · physis and techne

    8 What is the difference between physis and techne?

    1. Neither word names a field of study. They name two kinds of thing in the world.

    2. Physis is the origin and centre of all activity, and it implies movement, which is why it answers the question about what causes things to move. Techne is not on its own a part of nature. Schadewaldt's whole complaint is that modern science pulled these two apart.

    3. Reduction to the measurable is the modern move Schadewaldt is attacking, so this puts his own target into his vocabulary.

    4. No part of the lecture maps them onto body and soul.

    One of the two is where everything comes from. The other needs a person involved.

  9. What Wolfgang said

    9 Schadewaldt says the modern conception of nature has been "extraordinarily successful" and has cost us something. What is his complaint?

    1. His argument is about the conception of nature itself, before anyone applies it.

    2. A plausible thing for a classicist to say, and not what he says. The loss he names is in the concept itself.

    3. His word is dualism, meaning a split into two things that the Greeks held as one. The Greek physis held form and motion, law and life, causality and purpose together. Keeping only the calculable half works extremely well and drops the rest.

    4. A real problem, and a different one. Watch a thousand swans, see that every one is white, and conclude that all swans are white. The conclusion covers every swan there has ever been, and you looked at a thousand, so the thousand can never make it safe. Europeans believed exactly that until a Dutch expedition reached Western Australia in 1697 and found black ones. Reasoning upward from particular cases to a general law is called induction, and that gap is the problem of induction. It bites Bacon, whose method runs on it, and it bites the idea that knowledge is stacked like a brick wall on a bottom course that needs nothing under it, which is called foundationalism.

    He praises the results in the same sentence as the complaint, so look for what got left out.

  10. What Wolfgang said

    10 What does Schadewaldt propose as the way out of that dualism?

    1. He calls the calculable approach extraordinarily successful. He wants to add to it.

    2. Nothing in the lecture or the text proposes a change of authority.

    3. The split he is attacking runs between nature and techne, so swapping one Greek word for another does not close it.

    4. The dualism puts the observer outside what is observed. His fix is to put the observer back inside it. This is the sentence to carry into week 5 and 6, because feminist epistemology makes the same move and gives it a different name.

    A dualism separates two things. Which two does he most want rejoined?

  11. Who Francis Bacon is

    1. Empiricism means the senses are the original source of knowledge. "Father of empiricism" is her phrase, and it is the one to have ready. His slogan is knowledge is power, and she stressed it means power over the physical world.

    2. That is Galileo, 1564 to 1642, whom she used as her contrast to Bacon's complaint about Aristotle.

    3. That is Rousseau, 1712 to 1778.

    4. That is Lynn White Jr.

    The word for his position comes from the Greek empeiria, experience.

  12. Which theory matches which philosopher

    12 In Bacon's simile, the ant, the spider and the bee stand for three ways of working. Who is the spider?

    1. That is the ant. Bacon calls them the empirics: they collect and never convert.

    2. That is the bee, and it is the one Bacon wants you to be.

    3. The dogmatists, in Bacon's word. The insult is precise: they produce elaborate systems out of themselves and take nothing in from the world. Ant collects and does not think, spider thinks and does not collect, bee does both.

    4. A real Bacon complaint, and a separate one. He said that in Greece and Rome natural philosophy was left to perform the role of a servant.

    Where does a spider's web physically come from?

  13. Bacon against Aristotle

    13 What exactly was Bacon's problem with the Aristotelian worldview?

    1. Backwards. Bacon is the one insisting on observation.

    2. If everything already has a purpose built in, your job is to receive the order. Bacon wants you to go and get it, by experiment, which is why knowledge turns into power over the physical world. The contrast she used was Galileo, who found what the planets do by looking.

    3. That is Plato's position, and this is the standard mix-up.

    4. Bacon's objection is to the method Aristotle has. An absence of method is a different charge.

    Bacon was also critical of the alchemists. What do they and the Aristotelians share, in his eyes?

  14. The Middle Ages · Lynn White Jr.

    14 What is Lynn White's argument about technology in the Middle Ages?

    1. He argues the opposite: the religious worldview is the engine.

    2. The causal arrow in his argument runs from the worldview to the technology.

    3. This is the position his argument is written against.

    4. His chain: God is a creator and an architect, man is made in his image and told to rule the world as a creative cooperator, and Christian history runs in one direction toward a goal, so there is no time to lose. Therefore work, including manual work, is worship. Around 1300 this produces Deism, God as the clockmaker who sets the world going.

    Which way does the causation run, from belief to tools or from tools to belief?

  16. The circular jail

    16 Jeremy Bentham designed the panopticon. What is he most famous for?

    1. The trap, and a real "single best answer" case. He is famous to us for the panopticon because of Foucault. The professor's own slide says he is most famous for utilitarianism, and his panopticon was never built in Britain.

    2. Bentham held that the right action is whichever one produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number, and that doctrine is called utilitarianism. Her slide gave the two in that order. The prison reform is the link to the panopticon: he designed it as a humane replacement for what came before, which is exactly what makes Foucault's reading of it uncomfortable.

    3. Not a claim made in the lecture.

    4. Foucault, who builds it on Bacon's knowledge is power.

  17. The circular jail

    17 A ring of cells, a tower at the centre, and wide windows in the tower. What makes the design work, according to Foucault?

    1. The defensible wrong answer, and the one most people pick. If the watching were genuinely constant the building would be an efficient prison and nothing more interesting.

    2. The cells are divided from one another. The inmate is alone and seen, and not part of a group.

    3. Bentham's three words, which she listed on their own: visibility, unverifiability, and external power becoming non-corporeal. Non-corporeal means the power stops needing a body. Nobody has to be in the tower. The induced state of constant surveillance is produced by the uncertainty, which is why it costs almost nothing to run.

    4. That is the older public spectacle of punishment that Discipline and Punish says the modern prison replaced.

    If the watching were genuinely constant, the building would be an ordinary efficient prison.

  19. Foucault · History of Madness

    19 What does Foucault argue in History of Madness, 1961?

    1. Her examples were homosexuality and hysteria, and both are worth remembering, because each was a medical diagnosis for behaviour that broke a social rule. What was presented as objective discovery, that madness is mental illness, came out of questionable social and ethical commitments.

    2. The overstated version people reach for. His claim is about what the category is doing, and not that nobody suffers.

    3. This is the progress story he is writing against.

    4. His argument is about the classification. What causes the suffering is a separate question.

    Her two examples were homosexuality and hysteria. What do those two have in common?

  21. What is normal science

    21 What is normal science, for Kuhn?

    1. Normal describes the phase the field is in, and not the stature of the person doing it.

    2. Normal is not a compliment here, and Kuhn is not drawing a line around what counts as real science.

    3. A paradigm is the whole shared kit a field works inside: its theories, its instruments, its values and its assumptions about what exists. "Normal science is puzzle solving" is her sentence. A puzzle has a known shape and an expected kind of answer, and the paradigm supplies both. Her examples of paradigms, taken from the chapter: Newton's mechanics, Lavoisier's chemistry, Mendel's genetics.

    4. The tempting one, and it is the view Kuhn rejects. He denies that research makes steady progress. Normal science does accumulate, and the revolutions throw parts of it away.

    Kuhn's word is puzzle, and he chose it over problem deliberately.

  22. Incommensurability and the paradigm

    22 What is a paradigm?

    1. Too thin. A paradigm carries instruments and values and assumptions about what exists, and not a theory on its own.

    2. A crucial experiment is the thing Kuhn's account makes hard to believe in.

    3. A result a field cannot make fit, however hard it tries, is called an anomaly, and a pile of those is what eventually breaks a paradigm.

    4. Her phrase was "patterns for others to follow". Lavoisier's ideas about elements and the conservation of mass became the framework later chemists worked inside. Note the width of it, because that width is what makes incommensurability possible: if only a theory changed you could compare the two, and when the instruments and the values change with it there may be nothing left to compare them by.

    Ask how wide it is. If only a theory changed, you could still compare the two.

  23. Incommensurability and the paradigm

    23 What does incommensurability mean, and what were Kuhn's three reasons for it?

    1. The word means without a shared measure. Meanings change, so mass in Newton and mass in Einstein are two different things wearing one word. Indoctrination means scientists are trained into a paradigm, so they do not see from outside it. And historical context changes, so the question itself has moved. The critique she gave: many hold that the meanings problem can be overcome, and Kuhn softened his position and said communication stays difficult.

    2. This is Popper's shape, and none of those three terms is Kuhn's.

    3. Incommensurability runs between paradigms across time, and not between departments.

    4. A literal reading of the word, and she gave the counter-example herself: particle detectors stayed in use the same way while the theory changed around them.

    The word means without a shared measure. Her three reasons were about words, training and time.

  24. Why Kuhn's revolution is relevant

    24 Kuhn rejects Whig history. What is it, and what follows for how science gets told?

    1. Close in spirit and not her definition. The fault she named is about direction.

    2. This is the sentence she quoted from the chapter, and it is where Kuhn's revolution earns its place in a course about social values.     4. Not a position discussed in the lecture.

  25. The construction of kinds

    25 On the construction of kinds, what separates the nominalist from the realist?

    1. Both sides think classification works. They disagree about why it works.

    2. They are answering one question, about whether the categories were found or made.

    3. Kevin flagged this one as examinable in the middle of his notes. The nominalist's hard part is that we agree on classifications very easily, which looks like evidence we are tracking something. Their answer is that shared training explains the agreement. Behind it is the position that our categories are human handiwork, which is called constructivism, set against realism: any given theory is not the only one that could have been established, so a theory's success is not by itself evidence that it is right.

    4. The overstatement people reach for. The claim concerns our categories, and it leaves alone the question of whether there is anything out there.

    Both sides agree classification works. They disagree about why it works.
