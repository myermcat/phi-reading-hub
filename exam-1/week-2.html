  1. Plato · the text, the author, the dialogue answered before

    1 The allegory of the cave comes from which text, and who are the two speakers in it?

    1. The Apology is Socrates on trial. No cave in it.

    2. Book seven of the Republic. Glaucon is the one being walked through the cave. She said there is a question on the text, the author and the name of the dialogue, so hold three facts: Plato wrote it, the dialogue is the Republic, and Plato never speaks in his own voice anywhere.

    3. The Symposium is the drinking party about love.

    4. The Timaeus is Plato's account of how the cosmos was made.

    Plato wrote twenty-six dialogues. This one is his long work about the ideal city.

  2. Plato · what education is answered before

    2 Plato writes that education is not “putting knowledge into souls that lack it, like putting sight into blind eyes”. So what is it?

    1. This is the exact thing the quoted line rejects.

    2. That is skill at making or doing something, which the Greeks called techne, and Plato ranks it below the knowledge he is after here.

    3. Her phrase on the slide was that education is turning around. The eyes already work. They are pointed at the wall. Sight is no good if you are not looking in the right place.

    4. Nothing in the lecture supports this, and it is the opposite of a method of inquiry.

    If the eyes are not broken, what is the problem with them?

  3. Plato · the Kalliopolis answered before

    3 Beside Foucault's “panoptic utopia, the utopia of a perfectly governed city”, the professor wrote on her own slide: “think Plato, Republic, the Kalliopolis”. What is the Kalliopolis, and why is it there?

    1. Tempting, and the one to beware of. The Kalliopolis is a city, and the cave is a separate image.

    2. Plato's Kalliopolis is his own invention, and he is against Athenian democracy.

    3. Kalli means beautiful, so the most beautiful city. Foucault says the plague town has a political dream of strict division and regulation reaching into the smallest details of daily life. Plato wrote the attractive version of that dream.

    4. The realm of the Forms is transcendental, and it is not a city.

    • Kalliopolis: his beautiful city, run by philosopher kings, where the right education is what holds it together

    Kalli is the Greek for beautiful. Polis is the Greek for city.

  4. Plato against Aristotle · which theory matches which philosopher answered before

    4 Plato and Aristotle both use the word form. What is the difference?

    1. The right distinction with the names swapped, and the commonest way to lose this mark.

    2. The consequence is what to remember. Because Aristotle keeps form in the dog and the tree, he can go and dissect them, which is why she called him the first biologist. Plato's theory points away from the physical world.

    3. Neither of them means shape, and neither means a definition.

    4. The disagreement is about where the form is, which is a disagreement about what exists.

    One of the two could do biology. Which one, and why does that follow from where he puts form?

  5. Aristotle · what form answers answered before

    5 In the lecture, Aristotle's form and telos are offered as his answer to a question. Which question?

    1. That is the question put at the centre two thousand years later by Bacon and the empiricists, meaning the people who hold that knowledge starts in the senses.

    2. That is the Republic.

    3. The question the whole Greek picture is organised around, and she said so twice. Physis implies movement. Plato answers with the Forms in a realm outside ours, Aristotle with form in the thing and a goal it moves toward.

    4. That is Schadewaldt's question about physis and techne.

    • telos: the end a thing is growing toward

    Physis was defined in the lecture as implying one particular thing. What was it?

  6. Aristotle · what is critical to him answered before

    6 Telos is the term she stressed as critical to Aristotle. What does it mean?

    1. The word literally means end. Track it: Bacon attacks it as his central objection to Aristotle, Kuhn says history cannot be explained by what it led to, and she said explicitly that the idea of knowledge resting on a firm bottom layer, which is called foundationalism, works differently from telos.

    2. That is matter, which is the thing form is contrasted with.

    3. Closer to Plato's cave. The word itself comes from elsewhere.

    4. That is dialectic, and it is Plato's.

    • telos: the end a thing is growing toward

    The word survives in English in teleology.

  7. The era question that is really about the person answered before

    7 Which of these is true of Aristotle?

    1. Every word of this is Plato. She set the trap by giving both biographies together.

    2. Plato again.

    3. Aristotle is 384 to 322 BCE and Bacon is 1561 to 1626 CE, so this is about two thousand years late.

    4. 384 to 322 BCE, and she called him the first biologist. The non-citizenship is why he had to leave Athens at the end of his life.

    Two of these four describe Plato's life.

  8. Schadewaldt · physis and techne answered before

    8 What is the difference between physis and techne?

    1. Neither word names a field of study. They name two kinds of thing in the world.

    2. Physis is the origin and centre of all activity and it implies movement, which is why it answers the question about what causes things to move. Schadewaldt's whole complaint is that modern science pulled the two apart.

    3. Reduction to the measurable is the modern move Schadewaldt attacks, so this puts his target into his own vocabulary.

    4. No part of the lecture maps them onto body and soul.

    • physis: nature, meaning the source everything grows out of
    • techne: skill at making or doing something

    One of the two is where everything comes from. The other needs a person involved.

  9. Schadewaldt · where techne belongs

    9 Schadewaldt places techne between two other kinds of knowing. Which two, and what makes techne different from the higher one?

    1. Doxa is opinion and dialectic is Plato's method. Neither is in the quoted passage.

    2. The passage she quoted from page 29. Techne is productive knowledge, so it works on things that are still changing. So it ranks below episteme and above raw experience.

    3. Physis and telos are not kinds of knowing.

    4. Both English words translate techne itself, so this is circular.

    • episteme: knowledge of what never changes
    • empeiria: plain experience: you can do the thing and cannot say why it works
    • physis: nature, meaning the source everything grows out of
    • techne: skill at making or doing something
    • telos: the end a thing is growing toward

    One Greek word means plain experience. The other is the root of the English word epistemology.

  10. Schadewaldt · atechnia

    10 What is atechnia?

    1. The word names a failure of knowledge. Nobody in it is refusing to act.

    2. Her gloss on the slide was that atechnia is a false idea of the thing, held confidently. Note that it can be rich and diverse and still be a blunder, which is the uncomfortable part.

    3. Not a claim in the lecture.

    4. That is physis.

    • atechnia: making something confidently out of a false idea of how it works

    The prefix a means without. What is it without, given the knowledge in question still works?

  11. Schadewaldt · what Wolfgang said answered before

    11 Schadewaldt says the modern conception of nature has been “extraordinarily successful” and has cost us something. What is his complaint?

    1. His argument is about the conception of nature itself, before anyone applies it.

    2. A plausible thing for a classicist to say. He says something else: the loss he names is in the concept.

    3. His word is dualism, meaning a split into two things the Greeks held as one. Physis held form and motion, law and life, causality and purpose together. Keeping only the calculable half works extremely well and drops the rest.

    4. A real problem, and a different one. Watch a thousand swans, see that every one is white, and conclude that all swans are white. The conclusion covers every swan there has ever been, and you looked at a thousand, so the thousand can never make it safe. Europeans believed exactly that until a Dutch expedition reached Western Australia in 1697 and found black ones. Reasoning upward from particular cases to a general law like that is called induction, and that gap in it is the problem of induction. It bites Bacon, whose whole method runs on it, and it bites the idea that every claim rests on a lower claim down to a bottom layer that needs no reason under it, which is called foundationalism.

    • Induction: reasoning up from the cases you have seen to a law covering all of them

    He praises the results in the same sentence as the complaint. So the complaint concerns what got left out.

  12. Schadewaldt · the solution answered before

    12 What does Schadewaldt propose as the way out of that dualism?

    1. He calls the calculable approach extraordinarily successful. He wants to add to it.

    2. Nothing in the lecture proposes a change of authority.

    3. The split he attacks runs between nature and techne, so swapping one Greek word for another does not close it.

    4. The dualism puts the observer outside what is observed, so the fix puts the observer back inside it. Carry this past the exam: weeks 5 and 6 make the same move under new names, situated knowledge and standpoint theory.

    • episteme: knowledge of what never changes
    • techne: skill at making or doing something
    • Dualism: a split of one thing into two

    A dualism separates two things. Which two does he most want rejoined, given he keeps praising the results?

  13. The Greeks · Heraclitus

    13 She quoted Heraclitus, sixth century BCE: sound thinking is man's greatest power, and his highest aptitude is that he can say what is and “fashion creatively by hearkening to nature”. What was her one-line summary of him?

    1. Her summary line, written under the quotation. It matters because it is the background assumption Plato and Aristotle are both working against: there is an order out there, and thinking well means listening to it.

    2. The famous Heraclitus line about stepping into the same river twice. She used a different one.

    3. Protagoras.

    4. Socrates, in the Apology.

    Hearkening means listening. What does the quotation assume is out there to be listened to?

    2. Which means you can never quote a line as Plato's own opinion. It is a character's. She ran the Academy point alongside it: twenty-six dialogues, and a school.

  15. Plato · dialectic

    15 What is dialectic, in Plato?

    1. That is closer to the sophists, whom Plato is attacking.

    2. Her definition. It is a method, which is why it belongs in a course on scientific method: Plato's answer to the question of how you find out anything at all. The cave is the picture of it, and dialectic is the procedure.

    3. Hegel and Marx, and about two thousand years later.

    4. A conclusion Plato reaches. The question asks for the method he reaches it by.

    • Dialectic: question and answer until opinion has been sorted out from knowledge

    Two of these four are real meanings of the word from other centuries. Which one is the one she defined?

  16. Plato · philosopher kings

    16 Why should philosophers rule, and what does their education require?

    1. Plato's point is close to the reverse: the people fit to rule are the ones not hungry for it.

    2. Her quotation described them exactly that way. The geometry and astronomy matter: for Plato mathematics is a way to gain access to the abstract, which is the contrast she later drew with Bacon, for whom mathematics predicts what physical bodies will do.

    3. Not a claim in the lecture.

    4. In the allegory the one who has left is made to go back down.

    The subjects are the two that deal in things you cannot touch.

  17. Metaphysics · where the word comes from

    17 The lecture explained where the word metaphysics comes from. What is the explanation, and what does the field ask?

    1. The popular explanation, and she gave the other one. The supernatural never comes into it.

    2. A filing decision became the name of a discipline. Worth having, because it is a small concrete case of the course thesis: a category in science got its name from a filing accident. The subject's own nature had nothing to do with it.

    3. Not claimed anywhere.

    4. The word is Greek and predates the Middle Ages.

    • Metaphysics: the study of what exists, and of what things fundamentally are

    Meta here means after. After what, and in what sense of after?

  18. Who Francis Bacon is answered before

    18 Which description fits Francis Bacon?

    1. Empiricism means the senses are the original source of knowledge, from the Greek empeiria, experience. Its opposite is the view that reason alone gets you there, which is Plato. Bacon's version has teeth because he adds experiment.

    2. Galileo, 1564 to 1642, whom she used as her contrast to Bacon's complaint about Aristotle.

    3. Rousseau, 1712 to 1778.

    4. Lynn White Jr.

    • Empiricism: the view that the senses are where knowledge starts

    The word for his position comes from the Greek empeiria, experience.

  19. Which theory matches which philosopher

    19 In Bacon's simile, the ant, the spider and the bee stand for three ways of working. Who is the spider?

    1. That is the ant. Bacon calls them the empirics: they collect, and nothing happens to what they collect.

    2. That is the bee, and it is the one Bacon wants you to be.

    3. The dogmatists, in Bacon's word. The insult is precise: they produce elaborate systems out of themselves and take nothing in from the world. Ant collects and does not think, spider thinks and does not collect, bee does both. From Novum Organum I.95, quoted in full on her slide.

    4. A real Bacon complaint, and a separate one.

    Where does a spider's web physically come from?

  20. Bacon against Aristotle answered before

    20 What exactly was Bacon's problem with the Aristotelian worldview?

    1. Backwards. Bacon is the one insisting on observation.

    2. If everything already has a purpose built in, your job is to receive the order. Bacon wants you to go and get it, by experiment, which is why knowledge turns into power over the physical world.

    3. Plato's position, and the standard mix-up.

    4. His objection is to the method Aristotle has. An absence of method is a different charge.

    • telos: the end a thing is growing toward

    Bacon was also critical of the alchemists. What do the Aristotelians and the alchemists have in common, in his eyes?

  21. Bacon · his complaint about the ancients

    21 What did Bacon say had gone wrong with philosophy in Greece and Rome?

    1. Her wording. The charge is about rank: studying nature was treated as a junior subject that served the questions people really cared about. Bacon wants it at the centre, because it is the part that produces power over the physical world.

    2. Not his complaint, and the Greeks had a great deal of mathematics.

    3. A fair observation about Plato and not a Bacon complaint.

    4. That is his charge against the alchemists, the ants.

    He is making a claim about which subject was treated as more important than which.

  22. The Middle Ages · Lynn White Jr. answered before

    22 What is Lynn White's argument about technology in the Middle Ages?

    1. He argues the opposite: the religious worldview is the engine.

    2. The causal arrow in his argument runs from the worldview to the technology.

    3. The position his argument is written against.

    4. His chain: God is a creator and an architect, man is made in his image and told to rule the world as a creative cooperator, and Christian history runs in one direction toward a goal, so there is no time to lose. Therefore manual work is an essential and pressing form of worship.

    Which way does the causation run in his argument, from belief to tools or from tools to belief?

  23. The Middle Ages · Deism

    23 Around 1300, she said, the first ideas of Deism start to develop. What is the picture, and why does it matter for science?

    1. Once God winds the world up and steps back, the world is a mechanism, and taking it apart to see how it runs becomes a way of admiring the maker. That is how a religious worldview ends up licensing mechanical science.

    2. This is the view Deism moves away from.

    3. Deism is not atheism. There is a maker in the picture.

    4. That is closer to Aristotle, and it is what Christian thought rejected.

    • Deism: God as a clockmaker who set the world going and stepped back

    The usual image for it is a piece of machinery that keeps running after you wind it.

His line that barbarous people are those who lack virtue is tied straight back to Plato, Socrates and Aristotle.

    2. Bacon.

    3. Comte, from the week 2 packet.

  25. The era question answered before

    25 Put these four in the order they worked, earliest first: Bentham, Copernicus, Rousseau, Bacon.

    1. Copernicus dies in 1543, eighteen years before Bacon is born.

    2. 1473 to 1543, 1561 to 1626, 1712 to 1778, 1748 to 1832. Her detail standard means centuries are enough: Copernicus is late fifteenth into the sixteenth, Bacon straddles 1600, and Rousseau and Bentham overlap in the eighteenth.
