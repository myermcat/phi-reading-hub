Week 2: the Greeks, Bacon and the Middle Ages

Twenty-one questions on Plato, Aristotle, Schadewaldt, Bacon, Lynn White and Rousseau. Multiple choice, one best answer each.

  1. Schadewaldt · where techne belongs

    1 Schadewaldt places techne between two other kinds of knowing. Which two, and what makes techne different from the higher one?

    1. Doxa is opinion and dialectic is Plato's method. Neither is in the quoted passage.

    2. Techne differs because episteme concerns what is immutable and primary, while techne bears on what is mutable and in the process of becoming. The passage she quoted from page 29. Techne is productive knowledge, so it works on things that are still changing. So it ranks below episteme and above raw experience.

    3. Physis and telos are not kinds of knowing.

    4. Both English words translate techne itself, so this is circular.

    • episteme: knowledge of what never changes
    • empeiria: plain experience: you can do the thing and cannot say why it works
    • physis: nature, meaning the source everything grows out of
    • techne: skill at making or doing something
    • telos: the end a thing is growing toward

    One Greek word means plain experience. The other is the root of the English word epistemology.

  2. Schadewaldt · atechnia

    2 What is atechnia?

    1. Her gloss on the slide was that atechnia is a false idea of the thing, held confidently. Note that it can be rich and diverse and still be a blunder, which is the uncomfortable part.

    2. Not a claim in the lecture.

    3. That is physis.

    4. The word names a failure of knowledge. Nobody in it is refusing to act.

    • atechnia: making something confidently out of a false idea of how it works

    The prefix a means without. What is it without, given the knowledge in question still works?

  3. The Greeks · Heraclitus

    3 Heraclitus, sixth century BCE, wrote that sound thinking is man's greatest power, and that his highest aptitude is being able to say what is and “fashion creatively by hearkening to nature”. What does that assume about nature?

    1. Socrates, in the Apology.

    2. Her summary line, written under the quotation. It matters because both Plato and Aristotle accept it and then split over what follows. All three agree there is an order out there. Heraclitus says you reach it by listening to nature. Plato keeps the order and throws out the method: the senses show you shadows, the real order is the Forms in a realm outside this one, and you get there by dialectic. Aristotle keeps both: the order is in the things around you, as form and an end they grow toward, so looking at the world is exactly how you find it. That is the disagreement the whole of week 2 is about, and the thing nobody disputes is that there is an order at all.

    3. The famous Heraclitus line about stepping into the same river twice. She used a different one.

    4. Protagoras.

    Hearkening means listening. What does the quotation assume is out there to be listened to?

  4. Plato · who is speaking

    4 What has to be kept in mind when reading any line out of one of Plato's dialogues?

    1. Not claimed anywhere in the lecture.

    2. Not a claim she made.

    3. The opposite of her point. She said Socrates was a real person, and this is Plato's Socrates.

    4. Which means you can never quote a line as Plato's own opinion. It is a character's. She ran the Academy point alongside it: twenty-six dialogues, and a school.

    Count how many times Plato uses the word I in the Republic.

  5. Plato · dialectic

    5 What is dialectic, in Plato?

    1. A conclusion Plato reaches. The question asks for the method he reaches it by.

    2. That is closer to the sophists, whom Plato is attacking.

    3. Her definition. It is a method, which is why it belongs in a course on scientific method: Plato's answer to the question of how you find out anything at all. The cave is the picture of it, and dialectic is the procedure.

    4. Hegel and Marx, and about two thousand years later.

    • Dialectic: question and answer until opinion has been sorted out from knowledge

    Two of these four are real meanings of the word from other centuries. Which one is the one she defined?

  6. Plato · philosopher kings

    6 Why should philosophers rule, and what does their education require?

    1. Not a claim in the lecture.

    2. In the allegory the one who has left is made to go back down.

    3. Plato's point is close to the reverse: the people fit to rule are the ones not hungry for it.

    4. Her quotation described them exactly that way. The geometry and astronomy matter: for Plato mathematics is a way to gain access to the abstract, which is the contrast she later drew with Bacon, for whom mathematics predicts what physical bodies will do.

    The subjects are the two that deal in things you cannot touch.

  7. Metaphysics · where the word comes from

    7 Where does the word metaphysics come from, and what does the field ask?

    1. The field asks what the origin and nature of all things in the cosmos are, and how we investigate them. A filing decision became the name of a discipline. Worth having, because it is a small concrete case of the course thesis: a category in science got its name from a filing accident. The subject's own nature had nothing to do with it.

    2. Not claimed anywhere.

    3. The word is Greek and predates the Middle Ages.

    4. The popular explanation, and she gave the other one. The supernatural never comes into it.

    • Metaphysics: the study of what exists, and of what things fundamentally are

    Meta here means after. After what, and in what sense of after?

  8. Which theory matches which philosopher

    8 In Bacon's simile, the ant, the spider and the bee stand for three ways of working. Who is the spider?

    1. The dogmatists, in Bacon's word. The insult is precise: they produce elaborate systems out of themselves and take nothing in from the world. Ant collects and does not think, spider thinks and does not collect, bee does both. From Novum Organum I.95, quoted in full on her slide.

    2. A real Bacon complaint, and a separate one.

    3. That is the ant. Bacon calls them the empirics: they collect, and nothing happens to what they collect.

    4. That is the bee, and it is the one Bacon wants you to be.

    Where does a spider's web physically come from?

  9. Bacon · his complaint about the ancients

    9 What did Bacon say had gone wrong with philosophy in Greece and Rome?

    1. Her wording. The charge is about rank: studying nature was treated as a junior subject that served the questions people really cared about. Bacon wants it at the centre, because it is the part that produces power over the physical world.

    2. Not his complaint, and the Greeks had a great deal of mathematics.

    3. A fair observation about Plato and not a Bacon complaint.

    4. That is his charge against the alchemists, the ants.

    He is making a claim about which subject was treated as more important than which.

  10. The Middle Ages · Deism

    10 Around 1300 the first ideas of Deism start to develop. What is the picture, and why does it matter for science?

    1. This is the view Deism moves away from.

    2. Deism is not atheism. There is a maker in the picture.

    3. That is closer to Aristotle, and it is what Christian thought rejected.

    4. Once God winds the world up and steps back, the world is a mechanism, and taking it apart to see how it runs becomes a way of admiring the maker. That is how a religious worldview ends up licensing mechanical science.

    • Deism: God as a clockmaker who set the world going and stepped back

    The usual image for it is a piece of machinery that keeps running after you wind it.

  11. Plato · the shape of the journey

    11 Leaving the cave means moving upward, out of one realm and into another. What are the two realms, and what is the climb?

    1. No geography is involved.

    2. Age does nothing in the allegory. A grown prisoner is as chained as a young one.

    3. Plato calls the climb the journey of the soul. Sensible means what the senses reach: what you can see, hear and touch. Intelligible means what only thought reaches. The cave floor is the first and the sunlight is the second. Calling it a journey of the soul tells you the change is in the person and not in the scenery, which is why education is a turning around and not a delivery of facts.

    4. Agreement is not his test. A whole cave agreeing about the shadows would still be wrong.

    • Sensible: reachable by the senses: what can be seen, heard or touched
    • Intelligible: reachable by thought alone, with no help from the senses

    One realm is reached by the senses and one only by thought.

  12. Plato · what it feels like

    12 The freed prisoner is turned around, stood up and made to walk toward the light. What does the text say about the experience?

    1. He is dazzled and cannot see properly at first, which is the opposite of no difference.

    2. If it were pleasant, nobody would need to be made to do it.

    3. Learning as painful. This is the reason education needs a city built around it: left alone, nobody chooses the climb. It also explains the ending, since a person who found the ascent agonising has to be sent back down, and the ones below have no reason to believe him.

    4. A guide appears in the story and the pain is the detail the text dwells on.

    Ask why anybody would have to be compelled to do something good for them.

  13. Plato · what the cave is for

    13 The allegory of the cave is usually read as a story about reality. What questions is it actually built to answer?

    1. That follows later from the answer, and it is not what the allegory itself is working on.

    2. Which makes it a text about method, and that is why it opens a course on scientific method. The shadows are a picture of taking the readily available evidence for the thing itself. Everything after it in this course is another answer to the same four questions.

    3. No theology in the allegory.

    4. Not the subject.

    Ask what the allegory would be doing in a course about scientific method.

  14. Plato · the Academy

    14 Plato ran a school in Athens. What was it, and what is notable about who attended?

    1. The Lyceum is Aristotle's school, founded later.

    2. Worth holding for two reasons. It is where the word academy comes from. And the presence of women there is a useful fact to have when weeks 4 and 5 argue that science has been a closed shop, because the closure is not a fixed feature of intellectual life.

    3. The Stoa is where the Stoics taught, two generations later.

    4. It was a school with a site and a name.

    • Aristocratic: from the land-owning ruling families

    The English word for a scholarly institution comes from its name.

  15. Schadewaldt · sorting the examples

    15 A river, an oak tree, a bronze statue and a ship. Which belong to physis and which to techne?

    1. The test is whether the thing grew out of its own source or whether a person made it be what it is. The oak contains what makes it an oak. A ship is bronze and timber arranged by somebody, so it is a combination of man and nature. Schadewaldt's complaint is that modern thought treats the two as different in kind, where the Greeks saw the second as continuous with the first.

    2. A ship does not build itself and an oak needs nobody.

    3. Naming is not the test, and that claim belongs to week 4.

    4. The materials are, and the statue and the ship are not: somebody had to make them what they are.

    • physis: nature, meaning the source everything grows out of
    • techne: skill at making or doing something

    Ask of each one whether it would be what it is with no person involved.

  16. Aristotle · what follows from where he puts form

    16 Aristotle keeps form inside the object. What does that let him do that Plato's theory does not?

    1. Not the consequence drawn in the lecture.

    2. He is called the first biologist for exactly this reason. If what makes a dog a dog is in the dog, then the dog is worth examining. If it is in a realm outside this one, examining dogs tells you about copies, and Plato sends his students to mathematics instead.

    3. That is closer to Plato, who treats the senses as shadows.

    4. A later question. The location of form buys him something else.

    If the thing that makes a dog a dog is inside the dog, what is now worth doing to dogs?

  17. Aristotle · what explaining consists of

    17 If every thing has a telos, an end it grows toward, what does it take to explain something?

    1. That is Plato's kind of explanation.

    2. Material is one part of an Aristotelian account. It cannot be the whole of it, because the same bronze makes a statue or a bell.

    3. Explanation by purpose. An acorn is explained by oak. This is the habit Bacon attacks: if a thing already carries its own purpose, the knower waits to receive it. And it is the one Kuhn rules out of history, because an event cannot be explained by what it later turned into.

    4. Explanation by law arrives with the new science of the 1600s.

    • telos: the end a thing is growing toward

    Picture explaining an acorn to somebody who has never seen one.

  18. Schadewaldt · the accounting

    18 Schadewaldt sets out what reducing nature to what is calculable has bought, and what it has cost. What are the two sides?

    1. The second half is the reverse of his position.

    2. Neither item is in his accounting.

    3. He is far more generous than that, and the generosity is what makes the complaint land.

    4. Both halves are his. He will not argue that the method fails, because it plainly works. His claim is that something was dropped on the way and nobody can price it. An argument that concedes the opposition's best evidence first is much harder to dismiss.

    He praises the results in the same breath as the complaint, and the praise is not faint.

  19. Schadewaldt · which pairs came apart

    19 The dualism Schadewaldt describes split several pairs of things that the Greeks had held together. Which pairs?

    1. Four pairs, and the last one is the one that matters most for this course. Once the observer counts as subject and the world as object, the person doing the looking stands outside what is looked at, and his fix is to put them back in. Weeks 5 and 6 make the same move under the name standpoint theory.

    2. A modern set of distinctions and not his.

    3. A real split, named by Hume. His list is a different one.

    4. Real splits in the history of philosophy and not the ones he lists.

    • Extension: taking up space. The technical word for physical stuff, used as the opposite of thinking
    • Dualism: a split of one thing into two

    One of the four pairs names the observer and the thing observed.

  20. Rousseau · what follows from virtue

    20 Rousseau writes that barbarous people are those who lack virtue. Given that he also says progress comes only through education, what does the pair commit him to?

    1. The reverse: education is his remedy.

    2. He makes education the only route to progress.

    3. He is sceptical about exactly that measure.

    4. Which is why he can be inside the Enlightenment and against its favourite claim. Progress in the sciences and arts is not progress at all unless the people come out better. He points back to Plato, Socrates and Aristotle because they tied education to character.

    Put the two claims together and ask what an advanced society with no virtue would count as.

  21. The era question · the Greeks

    21 Put these four in order, earliest first: Aristotle, Heraclitus, Homer, Plato.

    1. Aristotle was Plato's student, so Plato comes first.

    2. Heraclitus is sixth century BCE, well before Plato.

    3. Homer eighth century BCE, Heraclitus sixth, Plato 428 to 348, Aristotle 384 to 322. The two philosophers overlap by about thirty years, since Aristotle studied under Plato. The detail standard here is centuries: Homer is first, Heraclitus is two hundred years later, and the other two are a pair at the end.

    4. Homer is two centuries before Heraclitus.

    Two of the four overlap in time, and one taught the other.
