  1. Foucault · Discipline and Punish

    1 Discipline and Punish, 1975, is described as a genealogical study of what?

    1. His account runs the other way. The modern method is gentler, and that is what he finds worth explaining.

    2. Genealogical means tracing how a practice came to be, without assuming it was an improvement. The puzzle he sets himself: punishment really did get gentler, so what is the new method doing that the old one could not?

    3. The book ranges across Europe and is not a legal history.

    4. A twentieth-century policy question, and not his.

    The word gentler is in her note, and it is the thing he finds suspicious.

  2. Foucault · power-knowledge

    2 Foucault says hierarchical observation leads to power-knowledge, which he glosses as “the development of force and the establishment of truth”. Whose slogan is he building on?

    1. Plato grounds power in knowledge of the Forms, which is a different relation: knowing comes first and earns the right to rule.

    2. Not a claim about power.

    3. Bacon meant power over the physical world, and he meant it as a promise. Foucault keeps the identity and turns it into an accusation: power and knowledge have the same goal, which is controlling what we know. Same three words, opposite verdict.

    4. Bentham held that the right action is whichever one produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number, and the name for that doctrine is utilitarianism. It says what an institution should aim at. It says nothing about whether knowing someone and controlling them are the same act.

    The phrase is three words long and it belongs to someone from week 2.

  3. Foucault · docile bodies

    3 What is a docile body?

    1. Docility here is about usefulness. Remorse is a separate matter.

    2. The list matters, because it shows how far past the prison the argument reaches. The same technique runs in a barracks, a factory and a classroom. Discipline produces docile bodies, which is why the prison is a model and not an exception.

    3. Discipline makes the body more capable.

    4. Close, and it misses the production. The point is what makes the body that way.

    Her list of examples had four items and only one of them was a prisoner.

  4. Foucault · the body as machine

    4 The Stanford Encyclopedia passage on her slide says the human body becomes a machine whose functioning could be optimized, calculated and improved. How?

    1. No surgery in the passage.

    2. Measuring every person against a norm and sorting them by it is what she calls normalization, and it is a real part of the lecture. It is not the mechanism described in this quoted passage.

    3. Break down, analyse, recompose. Think of a drill sergeant decomposing a rifle reload into numbered motions, or a factory time-and-motion study. The body is treated as something that can be taken apart on paper and reassembled better.

    4. That belongs to the older regime of punishment the book describes the end of.

    Three verbs in sequence, and the same three an engineer would use on a mechanism.

  5. Foucault · normalization

    5 What is normalization, and what does Foucault say it produces?

    1. The everyday medical sense of the word, and not his.

    2. She called it the goal, which is the word to hold. Everything else in the apparatus serves it. Once a norm exists, every person can be placed against it, and the placement is itself a judgment.

    3. Not a claim in the lecture.

    4. Not his argument.

    She used one word for its status in the system, and that word was not technique.

  6. Foucault · normalization in practice

    6 Which were her examples of normalization at work?

    1. All Foucault subjects, and not the list she gave for this term.

    2. Institutions of law, and normalization works outside the law.

    3. Notice that none of them is a prison. That is the point of the list: the technique that was visible in the prison is running in a school timetable and a production line, where nobody experiences it as punishment.

    4. State functions, and not the ones she named.

    Her examples were all places nobody is being punished.

  7. Foucault · the plague town

    7 Panopticism opens with the plague town. What does Foucault say the plague is met with?

    1. Historically common, and not his subject.

    2. Not the response he analyses.

    3. The order is the response, and the last clause is the sting: the sorting makes the fear and the death worse. The plague is the occasion where a town gets to organise itself completely, and Foucault uses it to show what total organisation looks like when it is allowed.

    4. Not in the text.

    His answer is a single word, and the surprise is what he says it produces.

  8. Foucault · the political dream

    8 Foucault says the plague has a “political dream”. What is it?

    1. Not the dream he names.

    2. Two halves, and the second is the one that matters: regulation reaching the smallest details. A plague gives authorities a reason to say where every person may stand and at what hour, and Foucault's claim is that this is attractive to power independently of the disease.

    3. Architecture comes later in the text, with the panopticon.

    4. The opposite of the division he describes.

    This is the entry she annotated with Plato and the Kalliopolis.

  9. Foucault · spaces of exclusion

    9 What are the spaces of exclusion, and what is the problem with them?

    1. A policy complaint, and not his.

    2. Not discussed.

    3. Her example was the nineteenth-century psychiatric asylum. The binaries she listed: mad against sane, dangerous against harmless, normal against abnormal. The problem is that real and varied people get sorted by a two-way switch, and then treated as the side of the switch they were assigned to.

    4. Not his argument here.

    Her example for it was an institution from the nineteenth century.

  10. Foucault · the modern prison

    10 What does the modern prison do, in her two-part formulation?

    1. The second half is the part people forget and the part that carries the argument. Losing your liberty is the punishment. Having your nature written down and filed is the new thing, and it is what links the prison to the clinic and the school.

    2. The older religious model.

    3. The standard justification, and not her formulation.

    4. Division and branding is a different point in the lecture.

    One half is about what is taken away. The other is about what is written down.

  11. Foucault · the building itself

    11 In Foucault's description of the building, how many windows does each cell have, and why?

    1. The obvious design, and it would leave the cell too dark to see into.

    2. The detail is the whole trick. Backlighting turns every inmate into a visible silhouette from the tower. The architecture does the surveillance, which is why one person can replace many.

    3. This would make the inmate invisible, which defeats the design.

    4. The inmate has to be able to see the tower, because the uncertainty about it is the mechanism.

    Think about what you need behind a person for them to appear as a clear outline.

  12. Foucault · the royal menagerie

    12 Foucault writes that the Panopticon is a royal menagerie in which three things have been replaced. Which three?

    1. A tidy-sounding list and not his.

    2. A menagerie was a royal collection of animals, arranged so one person could view them all. Foucault's three swaps turn it into a prison: the exhibit is now a person, the arrangement is now by category, and the viewer is now a machine with nobody in particular at the centre. Furtive means hidden.

    3. Not his wording.

    4. Half right on the first, and the middle term and the third are wrong.

    A menagerie is a royal zoo. Ask what each of its three parts turns into.

  13. Kuhn · the break with positivism

    13 Thomas Kuhn's book marked a break with what the professor called positive doctrines. What was the positivist paradigm she described?

    1. A false friend. Positive here has nothing to do with good.

    2. Positive here names the doctrine that real knowledge comes only from what you can observe and measure, which is called positivism. And the whole shared kit a field works inside, meaning its theories, its instruments, its values and its assumptions about what exists, is what Kuhn calls a paradigm. Now take her three words one at a time. Non-contextual: where and when you did the work should not matter. Formal and standardised: anyone following the procedure gets the same result. Separating variables: isolate one thing and hold the rest still. Kuhn's paradigms attack the first of these directly, because a paradigm is a context.

    3. The view that knowledge starts in the senses, which is called empiricism, is the root positivism grew from. It is not the description she gave.

    4. Popper, who was arguing against the positivists.

    Positive here is about a kind of method, and the first of its three features is the one Kuhn destroys.

  14. Kuhn · where his influence is strongest

    14 Where is Kuhn's influence strongest?

    1. His training was in physics and his effect there was slight.

    2. Real influence, and not the field she named.

    3. Which makes sense, because his argument is a claim about how to write history. If you cannot explain a sequence by what it led to, the historian's job changes: you have to reconstruct what the old paradigm looked like from inside, as a working system.

    4. His reception there was largely hostile.

    His central methodological objection is to a way of writing about the past.

  15. Kuhn · Copernicus

    15 What did Copernicus hold, in the professor's own framing of it?

    1. True of Copernicus and not the framing she chose.

    2. A Baconian slogan, and the Copernican system was not driven by new observations.

    3. She called this the important framing, so it is likely to be the asked one. Note what it means: he was not following better data. He was preserving perfect circles, an old commitment inherited from the Greeks. A revolution launched in order to keep something old.

    4. Kepler, sixty years later, and it is the thing that finally broke the circles Copernicus was protecting.

    She flagged one of these as her framing and as the important one. It gives his motive. The conclusion is the familiar part.

  16. Kuhn · what Copernicus opposed

    16 Copernicus held the sun stationary and the earth moving. Whose worldview was he arguing against?

    1. Plato's theory of Forms is not an astronomy.

    2. Which is why the Greek picture had to break before modern science could start, and why Aristotle keeps reappearing as the thing being overthrown. Bacon attacks his method, Copernicus attacks his cosmos.

    3. Bacon is born eighteen years after Copernicus dies.

    4. The Church's astronomy was Aristotle's, so this misses the source.

    The same figure whose method Bacon attacks about eighty years later.

  17. Kuhn · the Scientific Revolution

    17 What did the Scientific Revolution of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries replace, and with what consequence for philosophy?

    1. The consequence is the one the whole course is about. Before this, studying nature was natural philosophy, a branch of philosophy. It also replaced the Christian focus of the European worldview and embraced utilitarian concerns, meaning knowledge valued for what it can do.

    2. The direction of the split is the reverse.

    3. Neither half happened.

    4. A real change of the period, and not her point.

    Ask what the people doing it were called before and after.

  18. Kuhn · foundationalism

    18 What is foundationalism, and what problem did she name for it?

    1. Not her definition and not her problem.

    2. Foundationalism is about what holds a claim up right now, and never about history. Take a real chain. You say this kettle will boil at 100 degrees. Why? Because water boils at 100 at sea level. Why believe that? Because every measurement anyone has ever made came out that way. Why does that settle it? Here the chain is meant to stop. A measurement is treated as something that needs no further reason behind it, and that bottom layer is the foundation. Every claim above it rests on a claim below, and the bottom one rests on nothing further. Her phrase traced back means traced downward along that chain of reasons. It does not mean traced backward through time, which is the separate idea in this same lecture called Whig history. The problem she named: the step from every measurement so far to water boils at 100 is induction, and no number of measurements makes a general law safe. So the one layer holding everything else up is the one layer nobody can secure.

    3. A pun on the word, and not the doctrine.

    4. Not discussed.

    The metaphor is architectural, and the trouble is at the bottom.

  19. Kuhn · foundationalism against telos

    19 She noted that foundationalism is not like one other idea in the course. Which, and why?

    1. Not the contrast she drew.

    2. Induction is named as foundationalism's problem, so they are not opposites.

    3. Two different pictures of direction. Telos pulls from in front: the end is already fixed and the thing grows toward it. A foundation pushes from below: nothing is fixed ahead and you stack upward. Worth holding, because Kuhn then rejects both as accounts of how science moves.

    4. Not a contrast she drew.

    One picture has the destination fixed in advance. The other has only a starting point.

  20. Kuhn · revolutions

    20 What happens in a revolution, in her wording?

    1. Popper's picture, and Kuhn's account makes the crucial experiment hard to believe in.

    2. A result a field cannot make fit, however hard it tries, is an anomaly, and disciplinary matrix is Kuhn's later and more careful word for a paradigm: the whole shared kit of theories, values, instruments and assumptions. Revision of the kit, plus the solving of the problem that would not go away. Both halves are needed.

    3. Kuhn's account has the old generation largely not changing its mind at all.

    4. Her particle detector example argues the reverse: instruments often survive the revolution unchanged.

    Two things happen, and one of them is to the shared equipment of the field.

  21. Kuhn · the critique

    21 What is the main critique of incommensurability she gave, and how did Kuhn respond?

    1. He never abandoned it.

    2. Incommensurability is Kuhn's word for two paradigms having no shared measure to compare them by. Semantic means to do with meaning. The critics' point: if a term shifted meaning, a historian can learn the old meaning, so the barrier is a translation problem and translation problems get solved. Kuhn retreated from impossible to difficult, which is a real concession.

    3. Not the critique she gave.

    4. Not her account.

    The critique attacks the first of his three reasons, the one about the meanings of terms.

  22. Kuhn · the particle detector

    22 Kuhn says two paradigms can have no shared measure to compare them by. She offered a counter-example: the same particle detectors were used in the same way before and after the theory around them changed. Why does that trouble his claim?

    1. How often revolutions happen is a separate argument, and the detector says nothing about it.

    2. The force of the example is that the instrument did not change at all. Nothing new arrived.

    3. Incommensurability means there is no common yardstick. The detector is a common yardstick: both paradigms accept what it reads and both use it the same way. So at least one thing survived that either side can be held against, and the two are not sealed off from each other.

    4. That is the theory-ladenness of observation, and it is an argument for Kuhn. Her example was offered against him, so this one points the wrong way.

    Incommensurability means no shared yardstick. Ask what the unchanged detector is, if both sides go on trusting it.

  23. Kuhn · her conclusion

    23 If Kuhn is right that science has no fixed method and no secure foundation, something still has to explain why it works. What did she offer, and which philosopher did she attach it to?

    1. The view the whole course is written against.

    2. Ad hoc means put together for the particular case at hand. She attached this to Wittgenstein: a set of practices can be held together by overlapping family resemblances with no single feature running through all of them. Success without unity, which answers the course's title question directly.

    3. Rejected under his view of progress.

    4. Stronger than anything she claimed, and week 4 is careful to avoid it.

    She named a philosopher alongside it, the one known for family resemblance.

  24. Kuhn · the modernity of science

    24 Indoctrination is Kuhn's second reason for incommensurability: scientists are trained into a paradigm and cannot step outside it. Which group does that leave best placed to break one, and what becomes of the other group?

    1. This is the same mechanism as Kuhn's second reason for incommensurability, seen from the other side. Indoctrination is what makes a paradigm hold, so the least indoctrinated are the ones who can leave it. She added that scientists attend mostly to recent findings and treat past research as low value, which makes the field look young and modern.

    2. A reasonable sociological guess, and the opposite of what she said.

    3. The reverse of her claim.

    4. She drew an explicit contrast between the two groups.

    Which group has had the least time to be trained into the paradigm?

  25. Kuhn · why revolutions are not progress

    25 Under normal science, progress is easy to talk about, because the achievements pile up and are plain to see. Why does Kuhn deny that a revolution is progress in the same sense?

    1. Frequency is not the issue.

    2. Her exact reason, and the contrast with normal science is what makes it clear. Under normal science progress is easy to talk about, because achievements accumulate and are apparent. A revolution adds and subtracts at the same time, so there is no single direction to measure. Oxygen chemistry gained a great deal and lost every question phlogiston could answer.

    3. Not his argument.

    4. Often historically true, and not the reason she listed.

    Compare it with normal science, where she said progress is easy to talk about. What does a revolution do that puzzle solving does not?
